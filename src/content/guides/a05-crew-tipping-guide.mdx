The short answer: budget around 10% of the base charter fee for the crew tip, with 10 to 15% the usual planning range in the Mediterranean and 15 to 20% more common in the Caribbean. The tip is discretionary, calculated on the base fee — not on the APA or taxes — and handed to the captain at the end of the charter for the crew to share. Everything below explains the norms behind those numbers and the practical details that trip people up.

Tipping is the fourth cost layer of a charter, after the base fee, APA and taxes. If you have not met the other three yet, our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) breaks them all down.

## Short answer

There is no fixed rule, but published guidance clusters tightly:

- **Around 10%** of the base charter fee is the most commonly suggested figure across broker and etiquette guides.
- **10 to 15%** is the usual planning range in the Mediterranean; **15 to 20%** is more common in the Caribbean and the United States.
- **5 to 15%** was the band in the MYBA 2018 guidelines for excellent service; the MYBA 2026 update removed the figure entirely and states gratuities are at the charterer's discretion and should never be requested.
- On very large yachts the percentage often drops, because 10% of a very large fee is already a substantial sum.

A tip below 10% is generally understood as a signal of dissatisfaction, so calibrate honestly rather than defaulting low. And the number is always discretionary — no contract we have seen makes it mandatory.

<Callout type="note" label="Tip on the base fee, not the total">
Calculate the tip on the base charter fee only — not the APA, not VAT, not delivery fees or extras. Once APA and taxes are added, the total bill can look much larger than the base fee, and tipping on the total would inflate the gratuity well beyond the norm. This is the consistent advice across the guides we cite.
</Callout>

## How tips are shared among the crew

The custom is simple and worth following exactly: put the full tip in one envelope and hand it to the captain on the last day. The captain distributes it among the crew.

Do not tip individual crew members separately, even if one person stood out. The crew works as a team — the stewardess refreshing drinks, the deckhands setting up water toys, the chef, the engineer you never saw — and singling people out undermines that. If someone was exceptional, tell the captain in a note with the envelope; captains remember, and it matters for the crew member's record.

On larger yachts the captain typically divides the tip by role and seniority according to the yacht's own system. You do not need to manage this. Your job is one envelope, one handover.

## Cash or card

Cash in an envelope, handed to the captain, remains the traditional method and is still the cleanest: no fees, no delays, no confusion about who receives what.

But cash is less dominant than it used to be. Two alternatives are now common:

1. **Wire transfer after the charter.** Several etiquette guides note this is increasingly normal — the broker or captain provides details and you send the tip in the week after disembarking. Useful if you do not want to carry large amounts of cash.
2. **Unspent APA funds.** If your Advance Provisioning Allowance comes in under budget, some guests put the remainder toward the tip. This only works if there is a surplus, and you should confirm with the captain that this is acceptable on your yacht.

What to do: ask your broker before the trip which method the yacht prefers, and prepare accordingly. Do not assume a card machine will be available on board.

## When to hand it over

Timing matters more than people expect. The norm:

- **At the end of the charter**, typically on the final morning or evening, after the trip is complete.
- **To the captain**, in an envelope, privately — not in front of the whole crew and not as a public performance.
- **After any final accounting**, so the APA settlement and the gratuity stay separate in everyone's mind.

Do not tip at the start in hopes of better service. It reads as a transaction, it puts the crew in an awkward position, and the industry guidance is explicit that gratuities should never be solicited — which cuts both ways.

## Regional differences worth knowing

Tipping culture follows the cruising region more than the flag of the yacht:

- **Mediterranean:** 10 to 15% is the usual range; published guides describe the Med as sitting at the lower end of recommended percentages. In Greece specifically, brokers commonly advise 10 to 15% of the base fee.
- **Caribbean and United States:** 15 to 20% is the more common range, and 20% is described as not unusual for American charterers.
- **Middle East day charters:** less formalised. A 2026 Dubai guide cites AED 200 to 500 per crew member on a full-day charter as common practice — roughly $55 to $135 at the dirham's peg.

If your group mixes nationalities with different tipping habits, agree on the approach before the trip, not on the last evening.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Tipping on the APA or the tax-inclusive total.** The single most common and most expensive mistake. Base fee only.
- **Tipping individuals instead of the crew.** One envelope to the captain. Always.
- **Forgetting to budget it.** The tip is real money — $5,000 on a $50,000 charter — and it is due in cash or by wire at the end. Build it into the trip budget from the start, alongside APA and tax.
- **Assuming it is included.** Unless your contract explicitly states a service charge is included, it is not. Ask.
- **Tipping early to buy better service.** It does not work that way and it makes everyone uncomfortable.
- **Leaving it to the last chaotic hour.** Prepare the envelope the night before disembarkation so the handover is calm and private.

One last point: if the service was genuinely poor, a reduced tip is the honest signal — but pair it with specific written feedback to the broker. Silence plus a small tip helps no one, least of all the next charterer.
