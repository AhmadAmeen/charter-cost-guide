An empty leg is the closest thing private aviation has to a standby fare: a jet that must fly anyway, sold at a steep discount to anyone flexible enough to take it. Discounts of 25 to 75% off standard charter prices are commonly cited — but the discount buys you someone else's schedule, not your own. This guide explains how empty legs work, what they really cost, and how to tell whether one fits your trip.

New to private charter pricing generally? Our [private jet charter cost guide](/private-jets/cost/) covers hourly rates and what quotes include.

## What an empty leg is

An empty leg — also called a deadhead, ferry flight or repositioning flight — is a private jet flying a leg **without paying passengers** because it needs to move between assignments. A client charters a jet one-way from New York to Miami; the aircraft then has to get back to base or to its next pickup. That return flight, flown empty, is the empty leg.

When you book it, you get the entire aircraft: the same jet, the same crew, the same cabin configuration as a full-price charter. The only difference is the economics — the flight was happening regardless, so the operator would rather sell it cheaply than fly it empty.

## Why they exist

Most private jet trips are asymmetric: fly out Monday, return Thursday from a different city — or never return at all. That mismatch forces aircraft to reposition constantly. Typical drivers:

- An aircraft returning to its home base after a one-way charter.
- An aircraft moving to pick up its next charter client.
- An owner's personal itinerary pulling the jet to a new location.

The logic is simple. The aircraft has to make the flight either way, so any sale that covers part of the cost beats flying empty. That is why the discount exists, and why it has limits: the operator is recovering costs, not running a sale.

It also helps to know what an empty leg is **not**. It is not standby: you book and pay for the whole aircraft in advance, with a confirmed itinerary. It is not seat-sharing: no strangers board with you. And it is not a distressed product — the jet, crew and safety standards are identical to a full-price charter. What you are buying is simply someone else's schedule at a discount.

## Typical savings

Published operator guides consistently cite **25 to 75% off** the equivalent on-demand charter price. Independent broker guides agree: one puts the commonly advertised range at 25 to 75% off the standard one-way price, while noting there is no promised discount — it depends on the route, the aircraft and how urgently the operator needs to reposition.

Typical empty leg prices run from about **$1,500 to $50,000+**, depending on aircraft and route (BlackJet). To set expectations honestly: this is never "cheap" in the airline sense. You are still chartering an entire aircraft — just at a meaningfully reduced price.

## Trade-offs

The discount has strings attached. Every one of these comes straight from published operator guides:

- **Fixed route.** You adapt to the aircraft's route, not the reverse. Need New York–Orlando but the leg is New York–Miami? Some brokers can price a small diversion, but the core routing is set.
- **Fixed date and time.** The departure is dictated by the primary client's itinerary.
- **One-way only.** You must arrange your own return — commercial, another empty leg, or a separate charter (our [jet card vs on-demand vs fractional](/private-jets/jet-card-vs-on-demand-vs-fractional/) guide compares the booking models).
- **It can change or cancel.** If the primary passenger alters or cancels their flight, your empty leg changes or cancels too. This is the risk that matters most.
- **Short booking windows.** Most legs are published 24 to 72 hours before departure. Planning further out is possible but availability is thin.
- **Standard catering.** On last-minute legs, expect good standard catering rather than a custom menu.

## Step-by-step booking

Published operator guidance describes a consistent process:

1. **Set your corridors.** Decide which routes you would actually take — think in corridors (New York–South Florida) rather than rigid city pairs. Nearby airports widen your options.
2. **Get alerts.** Brokers and platforms offer empty leg alerts by email or app. Set them for your corridors and check them promptly — good legs go fast.
3. **Review the details.** Route, aircraft type, departure window, and the total price including taxes and fees. Confirm the FBO (private terminal) you will use.
4. **Confirm passenger details.** Finalise your passenger list, baggage and any pets before booking.
5. **Book and get final confirmation.** Expect schedule confirmation and FBO details roughly 12 to 24 hours before departure — and keep your phone on.

## Questions to ask

- Is the quoted price all-in, including taxes and fees?
- Is the specific aircraft type confirmed, or can it be substituted?
- What exactly happens — refund, rebooking, credit — if the primary booking changes and my leg cancels?
- What are the baggage and pet allowances on this aircraft?
- Which FBO do I depart from, and what are the arrival arrangements there?
- How will you notify me of schedule changes, and how quickly?

<Callout type="tip" label="The golden rule">
Never build immovable plans around an empty leg. Book it when your schedule can absorb a cancellation — a flexible long weekend, not a wedding you must attend. Used that way, it is the best value in private aviation.
</Callout>

## Is an empty leg right for you?

Ask yourself five questions. Can your dates move by two or three days? Can you cope if the flight changes or cancels with a day's notice? Do you have a plan for the return? Can you use a nearby airport? Have you compared the price with a standard one-way quote on the same type of aircraft? If you answered no to three or more, book a standard charter. An empty leg saves real money, but only for travellers who can bend. Never build a wedding, a meeting or a connection around one.
