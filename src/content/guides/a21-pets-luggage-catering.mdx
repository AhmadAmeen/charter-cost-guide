Three of the most common first-timer questions about private jet charter have the same answer: it depends on the operator and the aircraft. Can the dog come? Will six suitcases fit? Can they do a vegan hot meal? There is no single industry rulebook — each operator sets its own policies, and each aircraft has its own physical limits. This guide explains how those limits work, what published guides commonly cite, and exactly what to ask — and get confirmed in writing — before you pay a deposit.

If you have not booked before, read our [first-timer's guide to chartering a private jet](/private-jets/how-to-charter-a-private-jet/) alongside this one.

## Why rules differ by operator and aircraft

A charter broker sells you a flight; the operator who actually flies it owns the aircraft and sets the onboard rules. Two operators flying the same route on the same jet category can have different pet policies, different catering standards and different baggage practices. The aircraft itself adds a second layer of variation: a light jet's hold is a fraction of a heavy jet's, and some cabins have no proper galley at all.

<Callout type="tip" label="The golden rule">
Declare everything unusual at the quote stage — pets, oversized bags, sports equipment, dietary needs, celebrations. The broker can then match you to an operator and aircraft that actually accommodate it, instead of discovering the mismatch after you have paid.
</Callout>

This matters because of weight and balance. Every aircraft has a maximum payload, and on a full flight with full fuel the pilot must make the numbers work. Published guides note that on a full cabin with a long fuel load, you can be asked to leave heavy bags behind. The earlier the operator knows your load, the better the aircraft match.

## Baggage: cabin and hold limits

Unlike airlines, private jets have no universal per-person allowance. The limit is the aircraft's hold volume and weight capacity, which is why guides always quote ranges by category and tell you to confirm your specific aircraft.

A few practical points behind those bands:

- **Weight often matters more than bag count.** One published guide cites checked bags up to about 50 pounds each as a common working figure, with carry-ons around 25 pounds — but stresses these vary by aircraft.
- **Soft bags beat hard cases** on smaller jets, where the hold is an awkward shape and every inch counts.
- **Oversized items need planning.** Skis, golf clubs, artwork and musical instruments can all fly, but they eat volume fast and may need special arrangements — mention them early.
- **Cabin access is a perk.** Many jets let you reach your bags mid-flight from an internal closet, so keep essentials (laptop, medication, change of clothes) in a small cabin bag rather than the hold.

The single most useful question: "What are the exact cabin and hold limits, in weight and dimensions, for the aircraft you are quoting me?" Get the answer in writing with the quote.

## Flying with pets

This is where private aviation genuinely differs from airlines: most charter operators welcome dogs and cats in the cabin with their owners, rather than in the hold. But "most" is not "all" — policies differ by operator and by aircraft owner, and some impose weight limits, breed conditions or outright refusals. Never assume.

What to arrange, step by step:

1. **Declare the pet at the quote stage.** Published pet-travel guides advise giving the broker the species, breed, approximate weight, number of animals and carrier details up front, so only suitable operators and aircraft are offered.
2. **Confirm the operator's policy in writing.** Ask specifically: cabin or carrier required, where the pet sits during takeoff and landing, any weight or breed limits, and any cleaning fee. One European broker guide cites pet fees of roughly €100–500 per flight plus cleaning fees of €50–250 where they apply — but many operators charge nothing beyond the charter itself, since you have booked the whole aircraft.
3. **Check destination entry rules early.** Published guides commonly list a microchip, current rabies vaccination (often with a waiting period after first vaccination) and a pet passport or veterinary health certificate as the usual document set, with each destination country adding its own requirements. Some countries require prior authorisation for pets arriving by private aircraft.

<Callout type="note" label="Rules change — verify, don't assume">
Pet travel rules move. One 2026 industry guide notes the UK stopped accepting GB-issued EU pet passports in April 2026, replacing them with per-trip health certificates. That is exactly why you should verify the current requirements for your route with your broker and your vet several weeks out — never rely on what worked last year, or on a friend's trip.
</Callout>

4. **Prepare the animal.** Guides recommend a familiar blanket, water in small portions, light feeding before the flight, and a carrier the pet has practised in — the carrier must let the animal stand, turn and lie down. During taxi, takeoff and landing the pet must be secured, in a carrier or on a short restraint, depending on the operator's rules.
5. **Large dogs need floor space.** Big breeds often travel on the cabin floor by a seat. Because a large dog counts toward the aircraft's weight-and-balance payload, your broker may suggest a larger cabin — another reason to declare early.

Service animals are generally accommodated, but share documentation early and request seating with clear floor space.

## Catering and special requests

Standard catering on many operators — particularly on smaller jets — runs to a tray of fresh fruit, a cheese platter, snacks and drinks, served by the flight attendant or co-pilot depending on aircraft size. That is the baseline, not the ceiling.

What you can arrange, if you ask in advance:

- **Full meals** from a set menu or from a specific restaurant, kept cold and delivered to the aircraft before departure.
- **Dietary requirements** — published catering guides confirm vegan, vegetarian, gluten-free and children's preferences are routinely accommodated when communicated in advance.
- **Celebrations** — champagne, birthday cakes, flowers and decorations can be organised through the operator or the FBO's catering providers.
- **Specific brands** — if you want a particular wine, spirit or snack on board, say so; operators will usually source it.

Two caveats. First, **extra catering usually costs extra**, folded into your overall quote — confirm whether your quote includes it or not. Second, **small cabins have small galleys**: on a light jet, expect beautifully presented cold food rather than a cooked multi-course meal. Match expectations to the aircraft, and put every request in writing with a deadline (24–48 hours before departure is a common working window cited by operators).

## Your pre-flight request checklist

Use this as your template when briefing a broker. If you can answer every line before requesting quotes, you will get comparable offers and no day-of surprises.

**Passengers and pets**
- [ ] Full passenger count, including children and any passengers needing assistance
- [ ] Pet species, breed, weight, number of animals, carrier dimensions — declared at quote stage
- [ ] Operator's pet policy confirmed in writing (cabin rules, fees, cleaning charges)
- [ ] Destination pet entry requirements verified with broker and vet

**Baggage**
- [ ] Bag count, sizes and approximate weights shared with the broker
- [ ] Exact cabin and hold limits for the quoted aircraft, in writing
- [ ] Oversized items (skis, golf clubs, instruments, artwork) declared
- [ ] Essentials packed in a small cabin bag (medication, laptop, change of clothes)

**Catering and onboard requests**
- [ ] Standard catering confirmed as included or not in the quote
- [ ] Special meals, dietary requirements and children's food requested in writing
- [ ] Specific drinks or brands requested
- [ ] Celebrations (cake, flowers, champagne) ordered with a delivery deadline
- [ ] Cabin crew requested if you want one (not all light jets carry one by default)

**Confirmations**
- [ ] All of the above acknowledged in the written quote or contract — not just verbally
- [ ] Operator's name and aircraft type confirmed (see our [jet charter safety checklist](/private-jets/charter-safety-checklist/))

The pattern across all three topics is the same: the flexibility of private charter is real, but it is delivered through planning, not assumed. Tell the broker everything, get everything back in writing, and the flight will match the picture in your head — with the dog settled, the bags stowed and the right meal on board.
