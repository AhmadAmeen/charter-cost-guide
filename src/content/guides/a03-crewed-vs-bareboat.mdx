Crewed or bareboat is the first big fork in yacht chartering, and it changes everything: the price, who is responsible for the yacht, what skills you need, and what your days actually feel like. This guide compares the two side by side with sourced costs, so you can pick with your eyes open.

If you have not chartered before at all, read our [first-timer's step-by-step guide](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/) first, then come back — the booking process is the same either way.

## The difference in one paragraph

On a **crewed charter**, you hire the yacht with its professional crew: a captain runs the boat, and on larger yachts a chef cooks and a steward keeps things tidy. You are a guest. On a **bareboat charter**, you hire just the yacht — you (or someone in your group) are the skipper, the navigator, the cook and the cleaner. One is a serviced holiday; the other is a sailing trip you run yourself.

A second published data point shows the same pattern on one boat: a 50-foot catamaran in the British Virgin Islands might bareboat for about $12,000 a week but cost $30,000+ fully crewed — the difference being two crew salaries, a chef's provisioning budget and a service premium (source: VISlandGuide BVI charter cost guide, 2026).

The yacht type matters too — our [catamaran vs motor yacht vs sailing yacht comparison](/yacht-charter/catamaran-vs-motor-vs-sailing/) breaks down how the choice of yacht affects space, speed and running costs for groups.

- **The weekly rate is only the start.** Fuel, marina and mooring fees, provisions, end cleaning (roughly 80 to 150 euros) and small items like outboard fuel sit on top. One 2026 cost breakdown puts fuel at 100 to 250 euros and marinas at 150 to 400 euros for a typical week on a 38–40 ft monohull.
- **A hired skipper changes the maths.** Published rates run roughly 150 to 250 euros per day in the Mediterranean (operator examples: 150 euros in Thailand, from 200 euros per day plus expenses in Croatia, 150–200 euros in a 2026 cost guide), and about $190 to $380 per night in the Caribbean. A skipper also needs feeding and a cabin.
- **The damage deposit is real money held, not spent** — refunded if the yacht comes back undamaged. A non-refundable damage waiver (roughly 100 to 250 euros) is sometimes offered instead.

## Skills and qualifications

This is the honest filter. A crewed charter requires no sailing skill at all. A bareboat charter requires a competent skipper in your group — and "competent" is judged by the operator and the country, not by you.

- **Licences vary by country.** Many Mediterranean countries expect an ICC (International Certificate of Competence) or an equivalent national qualification; without recognised qualifications, a skippered charter may be your only legal option in those waters. In the British Virgin Islands, by contrast, operators commonly vet you with a sailing résumé rather than demanding a formal licence.
- **Check before you book.** Tell the operator exactly what qualifications and experience your group holds, and ask what the cruising area requires. Do this before paying anything — discovering at the dock that you cannot legally take the yacht out is an expensive mistake. Where no formal licence is demanded, expect a vetting process instead: operators commonly ask for a sailing résumé covering your years of experience, the size and type of boats you have skippered, where you have sailed, and any formal training. If the résumé looks thin, they will ask you to hire a skipper or take a check-out sail with an instructor before handing over the yacht.
- **The middle option exists.** A bareboat with a hired skipper gives you the lower bareboat rate plus a professional on the helm. Some operators even allow a skipper for the first few days only, handing over once you are comfortable.

## Damage deposit and insurance

On a bareboat, you are financially responsible for the yacht while it is in your hands. Two mechanisms cover this:

1. **Refundable damage deposit** — held (often on a credit card) against damage, released after the charter if all is well. Operator examples range around 1,500 to 3,000 euros; one Thailand operator lists 2,500 euros.
2. **Damage waiver (CDW-style)** — a non-refundable fee, roughly 100 to 250 euros, that replaces the large deposit. It caps your exposure but is money spent either way.

Read what each option excludes: sails, keels, propellers and water toys are common exclusions. On a crewed charter this whole topic barely exists for you — the crew is responsible for the yacht, and your financial exposure is limited to the APA account and the contract terms.

Take the handover seriously. On embarkation day, walk the yacht with the base team and photograph every existing scratch, scuff and stain — inside and out, including the dinghy and outboard. Test the engine, the windlass, the heads and the electronics before you leave the dock, and make sure any fault is written into the handover form. Most deposit disputes are not about real damage; they are about pre-existing wear that nobody documented. Thirty careful minutes at the start protects a deposit worth thousands.

## Who each suits: the five-question decision list

Answer honestly. Your pattern of answers points to the answer — and beware of answering for the group when the group has not agreed. The most common disappointment in bareboat chartering is one keen sailor booking a skipper-less week that everyone else experiences as unpaid labour.

1. **Can someone in your group legally skipper the yacht in that country?** If no — and no one wants to get qualified — choose crewed, or bareboat with a hired skipper.
2. **Do you want a holiday, or a sailing trip?** If the idea is being looked after, swimming off the back and never touching a rope: crewed. If the idea is helming, navigating and anchoring yourselves: bareboat.
3. **Who cooks?** A crewed yacht comes with a chef on most boats over ~60 ft (smaller crewed yachts may have a cook-steward). On bareboat, the galley is yours — which is either freedom or a chore, depending on your group.
4. **What is the real budget?** Price both options all-in for your dates: bareboat base + fuel + marinas + deposit exposure + skipper if needed, versus crewed base + APA + tax + tip. The gap is real but smaller than the headlines suggest.
5. **How much control do you want?** Bareboat means total freedom over the itinerary — and total responsibility when the wind picks up or something breaks. Crewed means the captain makes the weather calls, which is reassuring and occasionally frustrating.

<Callout type="tip" label="Trying before committing">
Not sure you are ready to skipper? A bareboat week with a hired skipper is the classic trial run: you sail, you learn the boat, and the professional handles anything beyond your comfort zone. Many sailors do one skippered week before going fully bareboat.
</Callout>

One last consideration: group dynamics. A crewed charter keeps everyone on holiday footing; a bareboat charter makes the group into a crew, with watches, cooking rotas and shared decisions. Both are wonderful — they are just different trips. Pick the one your group will actually enjoy, not the one that sounds more impressive.
