Charter brokers look free to you, and in the direct sense they are: you do not pay them a separate fee. So how do brokers get paid? They are paid a commission out of the charter fee, typically by the yacht owner or operator side. This guide follows that money step by step — including how affiliate sites like this one earn their share — so you know exactly who is paid by whom before you book.

## The short answer

A charter broker's commission is normally baked into the charter price. Published industry sources commonly cite around 15% of the charter fee, with 10 to 20% the usual cited range. Because the commission comes out of the fee rather than on top of it, using a broker usually does not make your charter more expensive than booking the same yacht directly. Confirm this with each broker you use, because arrangements vary.

## Where does the commission come from?

Follow the money on a typical booking:

1. **You pay the charter fee** to the operator or owner, as advertised.
2. **The owner pays the broker a commission** out of that fee — commonly cited at around 15%, within a 10 to 20% range depending on the broker, the yacht and the deal.
3. **You pay nothing extra.** The commission is deducted from the charter price, not added to it.

This is why brokers can genuinely tell you their service costs you nothing: their pay comes from the other side of the transaction. One industry guide puts it plainly — the commission is taken from the amount the client pays for the yacht rental, and another notes it is deducted from the total charter price, meaning clients fund it indirectly through the fee.

<Callout type="note" label="Charter vs sales commission">
Do not confuse charter commission with yacht *sales* commission, which is a different market — sales brokers are typically cited around 10% of the purchase price, paid by the seller. Everything on this page is about charter bookings.
</Callout>

## Why is your price usually the same with or without a broker?

Yacht owners work with many brokers at once, and the commission is a standard cost of distribution for them — much like a hotel paying a booking platform. The advertised charter fee already assumes a commission will be paid to whoever brings the client. Booking directly rarely unlocks a discount equal to the commission, because the owner would rather keep the margin than reprice per channel.

There are two caveats worth knowing:

- **More brokers can mean a higher cut.** When several intermediaries touch one booking, published sources note the total commission can drift toward the upper end of the range. It still comes out of the fee, but it is worth asking who else is involved.
- **"Free" does not mean neutral.** A broker earns only when you book, and may earn more on some yachts than others. A good broker recommends the right yacht anyway; a mediocre one recommends the one that pays best. This is exactly why it helps to arrive informed — which is what our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) and [first-timer's guide](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/) are for.

## How do affiliate sites like ours get paid?

Sites like this one do not charter yachts. When we recommend a broker, we use an affiliate link, and if you book through it, the broker shares a portion of its commission with us. You pay nothing extra — our share comes out of the broker's cut.

To show you what that looks like in practice, here are the published terms of the Boatbookings affiliate program, taken from their official affiliate page:

- Commission is shared as **a portion of the commission on the net charter value** — the charter fee excluding extras such as the APA.
- The **minimum qualifying net charter value is 3,000** (euros, US dollars, pounds sterling or Singapore dollars).
- Referrals are tracked with a **30-day cookie**.
- Affiliates are paid **one month after the successful completion of the charter**, by bank transfer.
- On **cancelled charters**, affiliates receive half of the due commission, reflecting the administrative work involved.

(Third-party affiliate directories describe the Boatbookings rate as around 20% of the net charter value, but that figure comes from the directories, not from Boatbookings' own published terms, so treat it as their description rather than an official rate.)

## Questions to ask any broker

- Is your commission paid out of the charter fee, or is there any separate fee I pay?
- What exactly is included in the quoted fee, and what is billed on top (APA, taxes, extras)?
- Are any other intermediaries involved in this booking?
- How is the APA calculated, and what happens to unspent funds?
- What are the cancellation terms, in writing, before I pay a deposit?

A broker who answers these clearly, in writing, is usually a broker worth booking through.
