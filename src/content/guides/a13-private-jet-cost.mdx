The short answer first: a private jet charter is priced per flight hour, not per seat. Indicative 2026 hourly rates run about $3,500 to $6,500 for a light jet, $4,500 to $8,500 for a midsize jet and $7,000 to $13,000 for a large-cabin or heavy jet, before taxes and extras. Everything below explains what those numbers include, what they leave out, and how to turn an hourly rate into a realistic trip budget.

<Callout type="note" label="Indicative ranges">
Hourly rates move with the season, the specific aircraft and where it is based. Published 2026 guides disagree with each other by a few thousand dollars per hour in every category, so treat every figure here as a planning range and get an all-in quote for your route before budgeting.
</Callout>

## How private jet pricing works

You charter the whole aircraft, not a seat. The core of almost every quote is a flight-hour rate for the aircraft category, multiplied by the flight time for your route. Two things follow from this that surprise first-time bookers.

First, the per-person cost collapses as your group grows. A light jet at $5,000 per hour costs the same whether two or six people fly, so the per-seat cost for six is a third of the per-seat cost for two.

Second, you often pay for hours you do not fly. If the aircraft is based far from your departure airport, the operator must fly it to you empty — a positioning (or ferry) flight — and that time is usually billed. A two-hour trip can easily involve three or more billed hours once positioning is added. Ask every operator how many hours you are being charged for and why.

Smaller turboprops and very light jets can run lower (roughly $1,800 to $3,500 per hour in the same guides), while ultra-long-range aircraft start around $13,000 per hour and climb past $20,000. In the UK and Europe, quotes often come in pounds and published guides note that Western European rates run higher than US domestic ones because of fees and regulation.

## What is included — and what costs extra

A standard quote generally covers the aircraft, the crew, fuel for the planned route, landing and handling fees, basic catering and passenger insurance. What it often does not cover:

- **Positioning flights** — the empty legs to get the aircraft to you (and sometimes back).
- **Crew overnights** — if your trip strands the crew away from base.
- **International handling and customs** — can add a few thousand dollars on cross-border trips.
- **Premium catering, ground transport and Wi-Fi** — usually itemised separately.
- **Taxes** — such as air transportation taxes on domestic flights, which vary by country.
- **De-icing** in winter — billed at cost when needed.

One 2026 industry guide estimates these ancillary items at roughly 10 to 25% of the total charter cost. That is why the worked examples below are labelled "before fees and taxes" — the all-in number is higher.

## Three worked examples

These are illustrative, built from the hourly bands above. They show how the arithmetic works, not what any specific operator will quote.

**Example 1 — two hours on a light jet: $7,000 to $13,000 before fees and taxes.**
Two flight hours at $3,500 to $6,500 per hour. Add positioning if the aircraft is not local, plus taxes and any extras.

**Example 2 — four hours on a midsize jet: $18,000 to $34,000 before fees and taxes.**
Four flight hours at $4,500 to $8,500 per hour. A typical use case is a US transcontinental trip for up to nine passengers.

**Example 3 — seven hours on a heavy jet: $49,000 to $91,000 before fees and taxes.**
Seven flight hours at $7,000 to $13,000 per hour. This is the shape of a transatlantic trip for ten or more passengers.

<Callout type="tip" label="The comparison that matters">
Do not compare headline hourly rates. Compare all-in totals for the identical itinerary: same airports, same dates, same passenger count, with positioning, taxes and fees itemised. Two quotes $10,000 apart on paper can be identical once the fine print is added — or vice versa.
</Callout>

## How empty legs cut the price

An empty leg is a flight an operator must fly anyway — usually repositioning an aircraft after dropping off paying passengers. Because the alternative is flying it empty, operators sell these legs at deep discounts, frequently cited at 25 to 75% below the full charter price.

The discount comes with strings attached: the route, date and time are fixed, the trip is one-way, and the operator can change or cancel the flight if their schedule shifts. Empty legs suit flexible travellers, not fixed plans. Our [private jet hub](/private-jets/) will cover them in detail in a dedicated guide.

## How to get comparable quotes

1. **Fix the itinerary first.** Same airports, dates, times and passenger count for every operator.
2. **Ask for the billed hours.** How many flight hours are you paying for, and how many are positioning?
3. **Demand an all-in total.** Ask for taxes, fees, catering and ground handling to be itemised or included.
4. **Check the aircraft.** Get the specific aircraft type and its year or refurbishment date, not just the category.
5. **Read the cancellation terms.** What do you owe if you cancel, and what happens if the operator cancels?
6. **Compare at least three quotes.** With ranges this wide, a single quote tells you almost nothing about the market.

Charter pricing rewards shoppers. The spread between operators on the same route is routinely 20 to 30%, which on a $30,000 trip is the price of another holiday.
