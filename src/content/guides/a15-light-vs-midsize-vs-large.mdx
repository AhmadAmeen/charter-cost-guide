Choosing the wrong jet size is the most expensive mistake in private charter. Too small and you cannot fly the route nonstop; too large and you pay heavy-jet rates for a trip a light jet would have handled. This guide compares the categories by seats, range, cabin and hourly cost, then gives you a simple way to match the jet to your trip.

One caution before the numbers: the charter market has no single official price list. Brokers publish their own bands, and they disagree — sometimes by thousands of dollars per hour in the same class. Everything below is therefore compiled from multiple published 2026 guides, with the disagreement shown rather than hidden. Treat the bands as planning ranges and always confirm with an all-in quote for your route.

For the full pricing picture — what is included, what is extra, and worked trip examples — see our [private jet charter cost guide](/private-jets/cost/). The hourly bands below are compiled from three published 2026 guides that disagree with each other, so every figure is indicative.

Two smaller classes sit below light jets — turboprops (about 6 to 8 seats, 600 to 1,000 nm) and very light jets (4 to 6 seats, up to about 1,200 nm) — worth knowing about for short regional hops, where they are the lowest-cost way to fly privately.

## Seats and baggage

Seat counts are only half the story; cabin volume and baggage space decide comfort.

- **Light jets** seat 6 to 8, but the cabin is snug — limited headroom on some types, an enclosed lavatory on most, and baggage space for a long weekend rather than a ski trip. Ask for the cabin and hold limits in writing if you travel with bulky items.
- **Midsize jets** add meaningful room: 7 to 9 seats, more headroom, larger baggage holds. The step up clients notice most is not the extra seat — it is the extra space per seat.
- **Super-midsize jets** bring stand-up cabins and proper baggage capacity for 8 to 10 passengers on long trips.
- **Heavy jets and ultra-long-range** aircraft seat 10 to 16 with full standing headroom, lie-flat options on some configurations, and baggage for extended international travel.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Never book by seat count alone. Tell the operator your passenger count, baggage, and any bulky items (skis, golf clubs, pet crates), and ask which specific aircraft fits — not just which category.
</Callout>

## Range and typical use

Range decides the category more than anything else. A jet that cannot fly your route nonstop either makes a fuel stop (adding time and cost) or is simply the wrong aircraft.

- **Under 3 hours** (city pairs, regional hops): light jet territory. The most popular category for first-time charter clients.
- **3 to 5 hours** (US coast-to-coast, western Europe to the Canaries): midsize minimum; super-midsize is the comfortable pick.
- **5 to 8 hours** (transatlantic, US to deep South America): heavy jet.
- **8+ hours nonstop** (intercontinental): ultra-long-range.

Published guides agree that upgrading on short routes is the biggest waste of money in private aviation — a heavy jet on a 2-hour trip can cost triple the light-jet price for no time saving whatsoever.

## Below light jets: turboprops and very light jets

Two smaller classes sit below light jets and are worth knowing for short hops:

- **Turboprops** (about 6 to 8 seats, 600 to 1,000 nm): P3 Jets' 2026 figures put them at $3,900 to $5,200 per hour. Note the overlap with light-jet pricing — at this end of the market, quotes matter more than category labels.
- **Very light jets** (4 to 6 seats, up to about 1,200 nm): Simple Flying's 2026 guide lists $2,500 to $3,500 per hour.

For a 45-minute regional hop with four people, a turboprop or very light jet is usually the lowest-cost private option. For anything much longer, step up to a light jet.

## What the hourly rate leaves out

Every band on this page is a rate before the extras. Budget for:

- **Positioning flights** — flying the aircraft empty to your departure airport, often billed at the hourly rate.
- **Taxes** — including US Federal Excise Tax of 7.5% on most domestic charter flights (2026 charter cost analysis).
- **Fuel surcharges, landing and ramp fees** — varying by airport and route.
- **De-icing** in winter and **crew overnights** on multi-day trips.

These routinely add 10 to 25% to the hourly-rate arithmetic. Always compare all-in totals, never bare hourly rates.

## Cabin comfort: what actually differs

- **Light jets:** functional cabins; some types offer stand-up headroom, many do not. Fine for 2 to 3 hours; tiring beyond that for taller passengers.
- **Midsize jets:** noticeably more headroom and elbow room, plus larger lavatories and baggage holds.
- **Super-midsize and above:** full stand-up cabins, proper work areas and room to move on long flights.

If cabin comfort matters — a bad back, working en route, travelling with children — say so in the quote request. Two jets in the same class can feel very different inside.

## New vs old aircraft: why quotes differ

Two quotes for the "same" category can sit 20% apart, and aircraft age is often the reason. P3 Jets notes that a fifteen-year-old airframe can quote about 20% below a two-year-old one of the same type — same category, different cabin condition, avionics and reliability record. When a quote looks unusually cheap for its class, ask the aircraft's year (and year of last refurbishment). When it looks expensive, the aircraft may simply be newer. Neither is automatically the better deal; it depends what you value.

## One-way, round-trip and empty legs

How you structure the trip changes the effective hourly cost:

- **Round trips** typically cost 20 to 30% less than two one-way charters, because the aircraft avoids a second set of positioning flights.
- **Empty legs** — flights the operator must reposition anyway — are commonly cited at 25 to 75% off the full charter price. They suit flexible travellers only: fixed route, fixed date, and the operator can change or cancel.

If your dates flex, ask about empty legs before locking in a category. A discounted heavy jet can undercut a full-price midsize on the same route.

## Questions to ask before you choose

- What is the specific aircraft type, and what is its nonstop range with my passenger count?
- What are the cabin dimensions and baggage limits, in writing?
- How many hours am I paying for, and how many of them are positioning?
- What is the all-in total including taxes and fees?

Three rules of thumb to finish:

1. **Buy range, not size.** The lowest-priced jet that flies your route nonstop with your passengers and bags is almost always the right answer.
2. **Count baggage as passengers.** Six people with golf clubs can need the cabin of eight people without them.
3. **Get the specific aircraft in writing.** A category is a promise of a price band; only the tail number tells you the age, cabin and exact capability. Ask for it before you pay.

## Final check before you book

- **Get three all-in quotes** for the same itinerary and compare totals, not hourly rates.
- **Confirm the tail number** — the specific aircraft, its year and its refurbishment date.
- **Read the cancellation terms** on both sides: what you owe if you cancel, and what happens if the operator substitutes a different aircraft.
- **Ask about empty legs last**, not first. They are a bonus for flexible travellers, not a planning basis.
