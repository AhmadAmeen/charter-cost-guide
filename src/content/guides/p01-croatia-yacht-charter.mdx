Croatia is the Mediterranean's easiest first charter: over a thousand islands, short hops between them, historic towns to aim at, and the clearest published cost data of any eastern-Med destination. This guide covers what it costs by boat type, the 13% charter tax, who needs a licence, the classic routes with realistic sailing times, and when to go.

If you are still choosing between destinations, our [Greece vs Croatia vs Italy comparison](/yacht-charter/greece-vs-croatia-vs-italy/) puts the three side by side. For the full cost maths, see the [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) — or run your own numbers in the [yacht charter cost calculator](/yacht-charter/cost-calculator/).

## What a week in Croatia costs

Published samples put Croatia at the affordable end of the Mediterranean for the headline base fee. An 80 to 100 ft motor yacht samples $41,000 to $94,000 per week in Croatia (source: BoatCrowd, via our cost data), against $42,000 to $80,000 in Italy and $47,000 to $82,000 in France. Smaller boats scale down from there: bareboat sailing yachts run about €3,500 to €9,000 per week before fuel, and small crewed yachts start near $10,000 per week.

Those are base fees — the number on the brochure. On a crewed charter, the honest all-in is roughly 1.5 to 1.7 times the base once you add APA (25–40%), a crew tip of around 10%, and Croatia's 13% charter tax. Take a $50,000 base fee: 30% APA is $15,000, the tip is $5,000, and 13% tax is $6,500 — a **$76,500** total, or about $9,562.50 per guest with eight aboard. Try your own figures in the [cost calculator](/yacht-charter/cost-calculator/); the [APA guide](/yacht-charter/apa-explained/) explains how the allowance is settled.

## Taxes: the 13% charter tax

Published provider guides cite about 13% charter tax in Croatia, calculated on the base fee — against 22% VAT in Italy and 20% in France. That single line is a genuine saving on a like-for-like yacht: 13% of a $60,000 base fee is $7,800, versus $13,200 in Italy. Confirm the exact calculation with your broker for your itinerary, because tax rules can change and multi-country routes complicate the picture.

## Licence and skipper rules

Here is the short version: on a crewed charter you need no licence at all — the professional crew handles the yacht. On a bareboat charter, Croatia is one of the Mediterranean countries where you are generally expected to hold an ICC (International Certificate of Competence) or an equivalent national qualification, and this is enforced at the dock, not treated as a formality.

Two practical consequences. First, tell the operator exactly what qualifications and experience your group holds *before* you pay anything — discovering at embarkation that you cannot legally take the yacht out is an expensive mistake. Second, if nobody in the group holds recognised papers, the answer is a skipper, not a workaround: operator examples put a Croatian skipper at about €200 per day plus expenses, and a skippered week is still far cheaper than a crewed charter. We could not verify the current official wording from the harbourmaster's office while writing this guide, so treat the operator's written confirmation as the final word. Our [crewed vs bareboat guide](/yacht-charter/crewed-vs-bareboat/) covers the qualification question in full.

## Routes: Split to Dubrovnik, and the Kornati alternative

The classic first-timer route runs one way from Split to Dubrovnik, hopping down the Dalmatian islands: Hvar, Korčula, Mljet, then the walled city itself. Legs are short enough that you sail in the morning and swim in the afternoon — this is why Croatia suits relaxed island-hopping. The alternative is a round trip from Šibenik or Zadar into the Kornati archipelago: a denser, wilder national park of 89 islands, better if you want nature over nightlife.

What this means for you: no leg demands an dawn start, which leaves every afternoon for swimming and every evening in a harbour town. If you would rather sail less and swim more, tell the broker — they will trim the route to the four or five stops that matter most to your group.

## One-way delivery fees: is it worth it?

A one-way charter means the operator must reposition the yacht afterwards, and you pay a delivery or repositioning fee for it. Whether it is worth it depends on the itinerary: on Split to Dubrovnik, going one way saves a full day of backtracking up the coast — a day you would spend re-sailing water you have already seen. On a round-trip-friendly route like the Kornati loop, there is no saving to buy. Ask for the fee in writing before you commit, and weigh it against what that extra day is worth to you. ## Best months to go

The Dalmatian season runs roughly May to October. July and August are the warmest and busiest — and the most expensive. May, early June and September are the sweet spot for most groups: comfortable temperatures, fewer crowds, easier berth availability, and usually lower prices. October can be lovely but the weather turns less predictable, so keep the itinerary flexible. For month-by-month detail across all regions, see [best time to charter by destination](/planning/best-time-to-charter-by-destination/).

## How to book

Croatia rewards the standard playbook. Decide your dates, group size and boat type first, then ask two or three brokers for quotes on the *identical* trip brief — same week, same yacht size, same itinerary. Ask each one to break the quote into base fee, APA percentage, tax, tip guidance and any delivery fee, so you can compare line by line.
