The yacht charter cost question has a short answer and a long one. Short: a crewed charter usually totals roughly 1.4 times the advertised weekly base fee once APA, tax and tip are added. Long: the base fee itself spans from about $10,000 to over $100,000 per week depending on the yacht, and every layer on top varies by region and season. This guide breaks down all four cost layers, shows regional samples with sources and dates, and works through three complete examples.

New to the whole process? Start with our step-by-step guide to [how to charter a yacht for the first time](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/).

## The four cost layers

Every crewed yacht charter price is built from the same four layers. Learn them once and every quote becomes readable.

1. **Base fee** — the weekly hire of the yacht and its crew. This is the number in the advertisement.
2. **APA (Advance Provisioning Allowance)** — a deposit for the trip's running costs: fuel, food, drink and dockage. Typically about 25 to 40% of the base fee.
3. **Taxes** — local charter tax or VAT, added on top and varying by country.
4. **Crew tip** — discretionary, but around 10% of the base fee is commonly suggested.

Crewed charters are generally quoted in one of two ways: **All-Inclusive**, where most costs are bundled into the rate, or **Plus Expenses**, where the base fee is quoted and running costs come from the APA (source: YachtCharterFleet, data collected 13 October 2026). Most of this guide assumes Plus Expenses, because that is where first-timers get surprised.

## Three worked examples

These examples show the full arithmetic from base fee to realistic total. All are labelled as illustrative examples, not quotes — your itinerary, season and yacht will differ.

**Example 1 — Crewed catamaran.** Base fee 15,000 euros. APA at 25% = 3,750 euros. Tip at 10% = 1,500 euros. Total: **20,250 euros before VAT**. (15,000 + 3,750 + 1,500 = 20,250.)

**Example 2 — Motor yacht.** Base fee $50,000. APA at 30% = $15,000. Tip at 10% = $5,000. Total: **$70,000 before tax**. (50,000 + 15,000 + 5,000 = 70,000.)

**Example 3 — Large yacht.** Base fee 100,000 euros. APA at 35% = 35,000 euros. Tip at 10% = 10,000 euros. Total: **145,000 euros before tax**. (100,000 + 35,000 + 10,000 = 145,000.)

Notice the pattern: the realistic total lands at roughly 1.35 to 1.45 times the base fee. Use that multiplier for quick mental budgeting, then confirm the exact lines with your broker.

## What changes the price

Five factors move the total more than anything else. When a quote looks high or low, one of these is usually the reason.

- **Season.** Peak months cost more and the best yachts go first. Shoulder seasons can mean lower rates and quieter anchorages — ask your broker which weeks they treat as peak for your region.
- **Region.** Base fees, APA percentages and tax rates all move by country, as the regional table shows. The same yacht can cost meaningfully more or less depending on where it cruises.
- **Yacht size and age.** Larger and newer yachts command higher base fees; a well-kept older yacht can be strong value. Always compare the year of last refit, not just the year built.
- **Itinerary.** Long daily cruises burn more fuel, which comes out of your APA. Tell the broker your planned route and ask what APA percentage fits it — the generic 25 to 40% range is only a starting point.
- **Crew size and service level.** More crew and higher service standards raise the base fee — and the tip is calculated on that higher base. If two similar yachts differ in price, crew numbers are worth checking.

## Ways to lower the cost

- **Travel in shoulder season** when base fees soften and marinas are quieter.
- **Choose a sailing yacht or catamaran** over a motor yacht of similar sleeping capacity; fuel burn — and APA — is lower.
- **Keep itineraries compact.** Short hops between anchorages use less fuel than long daily passages.
- **Compare two or three brokers** on identical trip briefs and compare quotes line by line, not by headline rate. Our [charter quote checklist](/checklist/) shows exactly which lines to check.
- **Ask what is included.** Water toys, Wi-Fi and premium drinks are sometimes extra; knowing in advance avoids surprises, not the items themselves.

## All-Inclusive vs Plus Expenses: which quote are you reading?

Before comparing any two numbers, check which quoting style each broker uses — mixing them up is a classic first-timer error.

- **Plus Expenses:** the base fee is quoted on its own, and running costs (fuel, food, dockage) are paid from your APA during the trip. Most crewed yacht quotes work this way, and it is the model behind every example on this page.
- **All-Inclusive:** most costs are bundled into a single rate. It looks simpler, but read the fine print: premium drinks, special catering requests, dockage above a set allowance and crew tips are often still extra.

Neither style is automatically cheaper. Convert both to an estimated all-in total using the four layers above, then compare.

## How APA spending works in practice

The APA is not a fee — it is your money, held to pay your trip's running costs. Understanding the mechanics avoids end-of-trip surprises:

- **Before the trip:** you transfer the APA (typically 25 to 40% of the base fee) to the designated account, usually a few weeks before embarkation. Your agreement should say who holds it.
- **During the trip:** the captain pays fuel, provisions, dockage and similar costs from it. On many yachts you receive running updates; ask at embarkation how spending will be reported to you.
- **After the trip:** unspent APA is returned to you, supported by receipts. If spending exceeds the APA — unusual on a normal itinerary, but possible with heavy fuel use — you settle the difference.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Ask your broker two APA questions before you book: what percentage fits my planned itinerary, and exactly how is unspent APA returned? Get both answers in writing.
</Callout>

## Bareboat costs in more detail

Bareboat charter deserves its own arithmetic because the cost structure differs. The weekly rate for a sailing yacht (about 3,500 to 9,000 euros) typically covers the boat itself. On top, budget for:

- **Fuel** — you pay for what you use, settled at the end.
- **Marina and mooring fees** — nightly charges vary widely by port and season; popular marinas in peak weeks are the expensive end.
- **Damage deposit** — a refundable security deposit held against the yacht, or a non-refundable damage waiver if offered.
- **Skipper** — about 180 to 250 euros per day if you hire one.
- **End cleaning, bedding and outboard fuel** — small line items that are sometimes bundled and sometimes not.

Add these up before comparing a bareboat week against a crewed one: a 5,000-euro bareboat week can easily reach 7,000+ euros once fuel, marinas and a skipper are included.
