Yacht charters and luxury cruises are often mentioned in the same breath, but they are priced in completely different ways — and that is where most bad comparisons start. A yacht is hired per vessel. A cruise is sold per person. Until you convert both to a per-person, all-in number, you are comparing a weekly rental with a ticket stub. This guide shows how to do the conversion honestly, then compares what the money actually buys.

If you are still learning how yacht pricing works, read our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) first — the four cost layers below assume you know them.

## Pricing models: per vessel vs per person

A crewed yacht charter has one price for the whole boat: the weekly base fee, plus APA (typically 25 to 40% of the base fee), local taxes, and a crew tip of around 10%. Whether four or ten guests board, the vessel price barely moves. The per-person cost is therefore a division problem, and bigger groups get a better per-head number.

A luxury cruise works the other way: the fare is quoted per person, usually per cabin, and the ship sails whether your group fills ten cabins or two. Your per-person cost is fixed regardless of group size — but the fare is only the start. Port taxes and fees, drinks packages, speciality restaurants, shore excursions, spa treatments, Wi-Fi and gratuities are commonly extra, and on luxury lines these extras add up fast.

The quote process differs too. A yacht quote is built for your group: you brief two or three brokers on dates, region and headcount, and each returns a vessel price with the cost layers itemised. A cruise fare is taken from a published price list — the work is not getting the price but auditing the extras. Both reward the same habit: insist on the all-in number before comparing.

<Callout type="note" label="The honest comparison rule">
Never compare a yacht's base fee with a cruise's headline fare. Compare the yacht's estimated all-in total (base + APA + tax + tip) divided by guests, against the cruise fare plus every extra your group would realistically buy. Anything else flatters one side.
</Callout>

Two caveats. First, tax is not in these numbers — add the local charter tax or VAT for your cruising region (published samples cite 22% in Italy, 20% in France, 13% in Croatia). Second, never divide by more guests than the yacht sleeps: comfort, not arithmetic, sets the limit.

What moves the $70,000 up or down? The APA share depends on how the group actually spends: a group that eats ashore most nights and makes short passages will underspend a 30% APA, while heavy cruising and premium wine will push past it. The base fee itself moves with season and yacht age — the same trip in August on a two-year-old yacht costs more than in May on a ten-year-old one. Treat $70,000 as the middle of a range, not a fixed price.

## Common comparison mistakes

- **Comparing base fee to headline fare.** The yacht's base fee excludes APA, tax and tip; the cruise fare excludes port fees, drinks and excursions. Both numbers are starting points, not totals.
- **Forgetting the group-size effect.** A yacht for four looks wildly expensive per person; the same yacht for ten looks reasonable. Run the division at your actual headcount.
- **Pricing the yacht you want against the cruise you would settle for.** Compare like with like: a premium yacht itinerary against a luxury sailing, not against an inside cabin on a mega-ship — unless budget is the only criterion.
- **Ignoring who plans.** A yacht charter needs an organiser; a cruise needs none. If nobody wants the job, the cheaper option on paper can become the worse holiday.
- **Assuming the seasons match.** Yacht and cruise high seasons overlap but are not identical. Price both for your actual dates, not for "summer" in general.

## Cruise fares: how to compare honestly

We do not publish cruise fares here, because they move constantly and depend on cabin grade, sailing date and line. Instead, take the current fare for the cruise you are considering and add the extras your group would actually buy:

1. **Start with the per-person fare** for your cabin grade and sailing date, from the cruise line or a travel agent.
2. **Add port taxes and fees**, which are sometimes excluded from advertised fares.
3. **Add drinks.** If your group drinks, price the drinks package per person per day — or estimate bar spending honestly.
4. **Add excursions.** Shore excursions are a major cost on port-heavy itineraries; count the ones you would actually take.
5. **Add gratuities, Wi-Fi and speciality dining** at the line's published rates.
6. **Add flights and pre-cruise hotel** if the sailing is not local — apply the same to the yacht option for fairness.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Build the cruise total in a spreadsheet with one row per extra. Most people underestimate the extras by half — writing them down is the whole point of the exercise.
</Callout>

Only now compare: the yacht's per-person all-in estimate against the cruise's per-person all-in estimate. For groups of six to ten on a mid-size yacht, the numbers often land closer than the stereotypes suggest. For couples or groups of four, the cruise usually wins on price alone.

## Control, privacy and pace

Money is only half the decision. Here is what differs once you are on board.

**Itinerary control.** On a yacht, the group decides where to anchor, how long to stay and when to move — within what the captain judges safe. On a cruise, the route and port times are fixed months in advance.

**Privacy.** A yacht is entirely yours: your group, your crew, no strangers at the next table. A cruise is a floating resort shared with hundreds or thousands of other guests.

**Pace.** Yachts move slowly and anchor in small bays large ships cannot reach. Cruises cover more distance and offer far more onboard — shows, casinos, multiple restaurants, kids' clubs.

**Service.** A crewed yacht has a crew-to-guest ratio a cruise ship cannot match; the chef cooks what your group asked for. A cruise offers variety and choice across many venues instead.

**Effort.** A cruise requires almost no planning: pick a sailing, pick a cabin, show up. A yacht charter means choosing the yacht, the region, the itinerary and the provisions — rewarding, but it is a project. Our [first-timer's guide](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/) walks through it step by step.

## Who each suits

**Choose the yacht when:** the group is six to ten people who want privacy; the occasion is a celebration worth a private setting; the itinerary matters more than onboard entertainment; and someone in the group is happy to lead the planning. If the group has a natural organiser — the person who already plans the holidays — the planning burden stops being a burden.

**Choose the cruise when:** the group is small, or spans ages and tastes that need different activities; nobody wants to plan; per-person budget certainty matters more than privacy; or the destination is best seen from a large ship's route. Multi-generational groups, in particular, often do better on a ship where teenagers, parents and grandparents can each find their own day.

**Consider both when:** the group is large enough that the per-person math is close — then decide on experience, not price. At that point the worksheet above has done its job; what remains is a taste question, and taste questions are best settled by the group, not by a guide.
