The most common complaint from first-time charterers is not the price — it is the surprise. The advertised weekly rate is only the first of several layers, and the extras that feel "hidden" are usually just poorly explained: APA, taxes, tips, delivery fees and deposits that were always part of the deal. This guide lists every one of them, with published ranges and dates, so you can budget the real total before you sign.

For the full pricing arithmetic, see our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/). For who gets paid what along the way, see [how charter brokers get paid](/yacht-charter/how-brokers-get-paid/).

None of these is a scam. They are standard industry mechanics — the problem is only ever that nobody walked you through them first. Work through each section below with your quote in hand.

**Illustrative example, not a quote:** take a $50,000 base fee. APA at 30% adds $15,000. Charter tax at 20% adds $10,000. A 10% tip adds $5,000. A $2,000 delivery fee to start at your chosen port brings the all-in total to $82,000 — 1.64 times the advertised rate. Change the tax to 13% (Croatia) and drop the delivery fee, and the same yacht totals $71,500. The base fee is the least informative number on the quote; the all-in total is the only one that matters.

## Costs paid from APA vs billed separately

The APA confuses people because it is both a big number and not a cost. It is a deposit — typically 25 to 40% of the base fee — held to pay your trip's running costs: fuel, food, drink and dockage. Unspent money comes back to you with receipts. What surprises first-timers is the boundary: which costs come out of the APA, and which are billed on top of everything.

**Usually paid from the APA:** fuel for the yacht, generator and tenders; provisions (food and drink bought for the trip); dockage and marina fees; local cruising taxes and port fees incurred during the trip.

**Usually billed separately:** the charter tax or VAT on the base fee; the crew tip; delivery or relocation fees; premium extras you request (vintage wines, special catering, onshore experiences); damage beyond normal wear.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Ask your broker for the APA boundary in writing: exactly which cost categories come out of the APA on this yacht, and which are billed separately. Then ask how unspent APA is returned and on what timeline. Two emails now prevent one argument later.
</Callout>

One more APA subtlety: the percentage is not one-size-fits-all. Published guides put it at about 35% for motor yachts and superyachts and nearer 25% for many crewed catamarans, reflecting different fuel burn. Tell the broker your planned itinerary and ask what percentage fits it — long daily passages burn more fuel than a week at anchor.

## Delivery fees outside the yacht's home area

If you want to board somewhere other than the yacht's home port, the yacht has to get there — and that passage, sailed without you aboard, is the delivery (or relocation) fee. It is a named line on the contract, separate from both the charter fee and the APA, agreed before you sign.

Published 2026 rate-sheet data gives a useful sense of scale: across yachts that print a figure, the median lowest quoted fee is about $1,750, with the middle half falling between $1,000 and $3,000. Real examples from current sheets show how location-specific it gets: $1,000 for a US Virgin Islands pick-up and drop-off; $1,500 from St Vincent and the Grenadines to St Lucia or Grenada; $2,000 from the BVI to St Martin or St Barths, plus another $1,000 to add Anguilla; $5,000 for St Martin, St Barts or Anguilla. In the Bahamas, corridors are priced like a timetable: Nassau to Norman's Cay $1,500 one way or $2,500 return; Nassau to Staniel Cay $2,500 one way or $4,500 return (source: VitalCharters, September 2026 data).

Three ways to handle it:

- **Board at the home port.** No fee at all, and often the widest choice of yachts.
- **Ask if the yacht is already nearby.** If it finishes another charter close to your chosen port, operators sometimes waive the fee — especially if you enquire early.
- **Compare the fee against the alternative.** Sometimes paying $2,000 to start where you want is cheaper than the flights, hotels and lost days of starting where the yacht is.

## Extras: water toys, Wi-Fi, special catering

The brochure photo shows jet skis, paddleboards and seabobs; the contract may not include them. Common extras and how they are usually handled:

- **Water toys.** Some are included, some carry a per-week charge, and some (like jet skis) may need a local licence or permit. Ask for the inventory list with prices.
- **Wi-Fi.** Basic connectivity is increasingly standard, but high-bandwidth satellite internet for remote work can be an add-on. If you need it, say so before booking — the hardware varies by yacht.
- **Special catering.** Standard provisioning comes from the APA; premium wines, spirits, specific brands and elaborate event catering are usually billed extra. One operator's event packages, for example, list food-and-beverage minimums separately from the vessel rate.
- **Onshore costs.** Beach club tables, restaurant bills, taxis and excursions are yours, not the yacht's — the APA covers the boat, not your wallet ashore.
- **End cleaning and consumables.** On bareboat charters especially, check whether end cleaning, bedding and outboard fuel are bundled or extra.

<Callout type="note" label="Note">
"Included water toys" means whatever is on the inventory list, not whatever is in the photos. Marketing photos are sometimes shot with equipment that is not on board. Ask for the list.
</Callout>

## Cancellation and insurance

Cancellation terms are the most expensive fine print on the contract, because they decide what happens to large sums already paid. Industry practice (per published Caribbean charter guidance) is a deposit to confirm, with the balance due 45 to 60 days before the charter — and cancellation penalties that grow as the date approaches. Read the actual clause: what percentage is forfeited at 90 days, 60 days, 30 days, and what happens if the *owner* cancels. Owner cancellation is rare — it usually follows a sale or an urgent repair — but the contract should still spell out your refund and whether the broker must find you an equivalent yacht.

On insurance, two separate questions:

1. **What does the yacht's insurance cover?** Hull, third-party liability and crew are the owner's domain — ask the broker to confirm cover exists.
2. **What do you need?** Standard travel insurance often excludes chartered vessels or watersports. Consider a policy covering trip cancellation, medical evacuation and personal effects, and check whether it covers sailing at all — some policies do not.

If you are booking an event aboard, also confirm whose policy covers guest injury — see our [events guide](/yacht-charter/events-birthdays-weddings/).

## Final checklist

Print this and work through it against your quote, line by line, before you pay anything beyond the initial deposit.

- [ ] Base fee confirmed, with the quoting style named (Plus Expenses or All-Inclusive)
- [ ] APA percentage confirmed for *my* itinerary, with the APA boundary in writing
- [ ] Charter tax / VAT rate confirmed, and whether the quote is plus-tax or tax-included
- [ ] Crew tip expectations understood (commonly around 10%)
- [ ] Delivery / relocation fee confirmed or confirmed as zero, on the contract
- [ ] Water toys inventory received with any extra charges listed
- [ ] Wi-Fi capability confirmed if I need to work aboard
- [ ] Catering rules confirmed: outside caterer allowed? corkage? premium items billed how?
- [ ] Damage deposit terms understood (bareboat): amount, hold method, waiver option
- [ ] Payment schedule confirmed: deposit now, balance due date (often 45–60 days out)
- [ ] Cancellation clause read: my penalties by date, and owner's obligations if they cancel
- [ ] Insurance checked: yacht's cover confirmed; my own policy covers sailing and cancellation
- [ ] Everything above confirmed in writing, not just verbally
