There are three established ways to fly privately without buying a whole aircraft: book each trip as you go (on-demand charter), prepay for hours at fixed rates (jet card), or buy a slice of an aircraft (fractional ownership). The right one is mostly a function of how much you fly — but the boundaries are bands, not lines, and every provider draws them slightly differently. This guide defines each model, prices them from published 2026 guides, and gives you a decision framework.

New to private flying altogether? Start with [how much a private jet charter costs](/private-jets/cost/) and [how to charter a private jet for the first time](/private-jets/how-to-charter-a-private-jet/).

## The three models defined

**On-demand charter** is pay-as-you-go private aviation. You contact a broker or operator per trip, get a quote at market rates for that route and date, and pay for that flight only. No upfront payment, no contract, no commitment. This is the model our charter guides describe throughout.

**Jet card** is prepaid flight time. You deposit a lump sum — published guides cite roughly $100,000 to $500,000 for 25+ hours — and draw down hours at a fixed hourly rate. In exchange you get price predictability and guaranteed availability (typically 24 to 72 hours' notice), usually on a 12 to 24 month contract.

**Fractional ownership** is buying a share of a specific aircraft type — typically 1/16 to 1/8 of a jet. You pay a share purchase price (published guides cite roughly $500,000 to over $1 million, up to $10 million+ for larger shares), plus monthly management fees whether you fly or not, plus occupied hourly operating costs. Contracts typically run 3 to 5 years. In return you get consistent access to your aircraft type with strong availability guarantees.

Every provider publishes its own version of "who each model suits," and the hour bands overlap. We show the bands as published and flag the disagreements rather than pretending there is one true answer. Your routes, flexibility and tolerance for commitment move the real break-even point.

A few published data points behind the table. A 25-hour jet card on a midsize jet is cited at roughly $150,000 to $250,000 (VOLO Aviation). Jettly's comparison puts typical light-jet jet card rates around $6,000 to $8,000 per hour all-in, versus $6,500 to $8,500 for on-demand charter. Fractional's effective hourly rate at high utilisation is cited around $4,000 to $6,000 — but only after the share purchase and monthly fees are sunk.

The honest summary: on-demand has the highest per-hour price and zero fixed cost; fractional has the lowest per-hour price and the highest fixed cost; the jet card sits between on both axes.

In plain terms:

- **Fly occasionally (a few trips a year)?** On-demand charter. One provider guide notes occasional flyers at 8–12 trips a year typically pay 15–25% less per hour on-demand than on a jet card, because they are not funding hours they do not use — and they keep access to empty-leg deals.
- **Fly regularly (a couple of times a month)?** Jet card. Fixed rates remove peak-season price spikes, guaranteed availability removes the "is there a jet?" anxiety, and 25–50 prepaid hours match the pattern.
- **Fly constantly on predictable routes (100+ hours)?** Fractional. The fixed costs amortise, the hourly rate drops, and you fly the same aircraft type every time.

One 2026 cost analysis makes the arithmetic concrete: at **50 hours a year**, chartering ($300,000–$450,000) beats a 1/16th midsize fractional share ($400,000–$500,000 all-in). At **150 hours a year**, the positions reverse — fractional ($700,000–$850,000) beats charter ($900,000–$1,350,000). The break-even sits somewhere around 100 hours, depending on the provider and the routes.

## Questions to ask before committing

A jet card or fractional share is a five- or six-figure commitment. Ask these before signing:

1. **Do hours expire?** Many jet cards run 12–24 months with expiring hours; some are refundable or never expire. Get the clause in writing.
2. **What are the peak-day rules?** How many peak or blackout days per year, and what surcharges apply on them?
3. **Are repositioning fees included?** Some cards include positioning on qualifying routes; others bill it. This changes the effective hourly rate significantly.
4. **What aircraft am I guaranteed?** Category or specific type — and what happens on substitution?
5. **What are the monthly fees (fractional)?** Confirm the amount, what it covers, and whether it is payable even in months you do not fly.
6. **What is the exit?** Contract length, renewal terms, early termination penalties, and — for fractional — how the share is sold or bought back.
7. **Who operates the aircraft?** Broker vs operator matters for safety vetting; our [safety checklist](/private-jets/charter-safety-checklist/) applies whichever model you choose.

## Common traps

- **Buying hours you will not fly.** The classic jet-card mistake: 25 prepaid hours sounds modest until the year ends with 11 unused and an expiry clause. Be conservative about your real pattern.
- **Ignoring the monthly fees.** Fractional management fees of $8,000–$25,000 a month are payable whether you fly or not — $96,000 to $300,000 a year before a single hour.
- **Assuming "fixed rate" means "all-in."** Fixed hourly rates can still exclude catering upgrades, premium airports, de-icing, international fees and peak surcharges. Ask what the fixed rate excludes.
- **Peak-day lockouts.** Some cards cap peak-day access or surcharge heavily on the exact days you most want to fly (holidays, major events). Count your likely peak days before comparing cards.
- **Treating the bands as promises.** "25–100 hours suits a jet card" is a general guide, not a guarantee — a flyer doing 40 short hops faces different economics than one doing 40 transcontinental legs. Run your own routes through the quotes.

The decision, reduced to one line: if your flying is irregular, stay on-demand; if it is regular enough that price spikes and availability stress bother you, price a jet card against your last twelve months of trips; only consider fractional when the hours — and the capital — are genuinely there.
