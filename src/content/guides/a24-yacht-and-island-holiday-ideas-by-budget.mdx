"Yacht holiday" covers everything from a 3,500-euro bareboat week split among friends to a six-figure crewed motor yacht. The mistake is shopping by yacht first and budget second. Flip it: decide what each person can spend, see which rung of the ladder that reaches, then pick the yacht. This guide lays out the ladder with per-person arithmetic so you can do exactly that.

Why per-person math matters more here than in almost any other holiday: the yacht's price barely moves with headcount, so every empty cabin is money nobody spent well. A group of six paying for a ten-guest yacht is subsidising four empty beds. Get the headcount right first and the budget question answers itself — the tables below show why.

All base-fee ranges below come from published broker guides (sources at the bottom). For the full breakdown of what sits on top of a base fee, see our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/). If you are weighing a yacht against a cruise instead, our [yacht vs luxury cruise comparison](/planning/yacht-charter-vs-luxury-cruise/) runs the per-person math for that decision.

Read the pattern, not just your row: doubling the group from five to ten roughly halves the per-person cost on every rung. If your group is flexible on numbers, adding two more friends is the single biggest lever on per-person price.

## What each level feels like

Neutral descriptions — no rung is "better", they buy different things.

**Bareboat sailing.** You are the crew. Days are built around sailing the boat: planning passages, handling lines, cooking in the galley, sleeping in compact cabins. It is hands-on and social, and the lowest-cost way to spend a week on the water. It demands a qualified skipper in the group and suits people who want to sail, not be sailed. Evenings tend to be simple — cockpit dinners, a walk ashore — because the day's sailing was the entertainment.

**Crewed catamaran.** The most popular group option for a reason. A professional crew handles the boat and the cooking; the wide beam means a big cockpit, trampolines up front and cabins that feel closer to hotel rooms than boat cabins. Stable at anchor, slower under way than a motor yacht. The classic family-and-friends week: nobody works, everybody swims, and the biggest decision is lunch.

**Motor yacht, 80 to 100 ft.** Faster and longer-legged: breakfast in one bay, lunch in another island's harbour. Higher crew numbers, a chef cooking to your preference sheet, water toys as standard. You cover serious distance without long days. The per-person cost steps up markedly — this is the celebration-trip bracket, where the yacht itself is part of the occasion.

**Large motor yacht.** A private floating hotel with departmental crew: chef, stewardesses, deckhands, sometimes a masseuse or dive instructor. Itineraries bend around you entirely. Priced for occasions where the setting is the point — milestone birthdays, weddings, board-level offsites.

## Sample week at each level

These are sketches to show what the money buys in practice, not quotes or fixed itineraries.

**Bareboat.** A 40-foot monohull sleeping eight, sailing in shoulder season. Short passages between islands, anchoring for lunch, cooking aboard most nights, a marina every second or third night for water and showers. Per person for eight: roughly 440 to 1,125 euros base, plus fuel, marinas and provisions split eight ways.

**Crewed catamaran.** A 50-foot catamaran with captain and chef in the Caribbean. The crew sails and cooks; the group swims, snorkels and goes ashore. Dinners aboard or at beach restaurants. Per person for eight: roughly 1,875 to 5,625 euros base, plus the APA share.

**Motor yacht, 80 to 100 ft.** Greece in June. Morning passages of a few hours, lunch at anchor, afternoons on water toys, dinner at a harbour restaurant. Chef, stewardess and deckhand included. Per person for eight: roughly $5,125 to $11,750 base, plus APA, tax and tip shares.

**Large motor yacht.** A slow week along one coastline — no rushing, the crew handling everything down to laundry. The itinerary is whatever the group wants that morning. Per person for eight: roughly $12,500+ base, plus APA, tax and tip shares.

## Money traps at every budget

- **Bareboat: the marina bill.** Nightly fees in popular ports add up fast; anchor out more often if the group is comfortable with it.
- **Bareboat: the damage deposit.** Usually refundable, but know the amount and whether a non-refundable waiver is offered before you go.
- **Crewed: APA drift.** The APA is your money — heavy fuel use or premium wine moves it. Ask the captain for running updates during the week.
- **Crewed: the tip base.** The commonly suggested ~10% tip is calculated on the base fee, so a bigger yacht means a bigger tip before you board.
- **Every level: flights and transfers.** A low-cost yacht week with expensive flights is not a low-cost holiday. Price the whole trip door to door.
- **Every level: shoulder-season weather.** Lower prices come with a higher chance of losing a day or two to weather. Build in a buffer day rather than a tight itinerary.

## Bareboat vs crewed at the same per-person budget

This is the argument groups actually have. Take eight guests with about 2,000 euros each — 16,000 euros total — and see what it reaches.

**Top-end bareboat:** a 9,000-euro boat leaves roughly 7,000 euros for the week's fuel, marina fees, provisions and a hired skipper (about 180 to 250 euros per day, so roughly 1,260 to 1,750 for the week split eight ways). Comfortable — the group sails itself and the budget covers everything with room to spare.

**Entry-level crewed catamaran:** a 15,000-euro base fee leaves only about 1,000 euros toward the APA, but a 25% APA on that boat is 3,750 euros — before tax and tip. This group would realistically need around 2,400 to 2,500 euros each to do the crewed week properly.

The honest conclusion: at the same per-person number, bareboat buys comfort and crewed buys service — you cannot have both at the boundary. If the group values being looked after over sailing itself, the answer is to raise the budget or add two more guests, not to squeeze a crewed week into a bareboat number.

## When to step up (or down) a rung

- **Step up** if the itinerary needs speed, the group wants full service, or the occasion justifies the setting.
- **Step down** if the group enjoys sailing itself, the itinerary is compact, or the per-person number only works one rung lower.
- **Stay put** if the numbers work and the style fits. The ladder has no prestige ranking — the right rung is the one that matches the group, not the one that impresses it.

## How to pick your level

1. **Set the per-person budget first**, all-in (base + APA + tax + tip), not base fee.
2. **Count confirmed guests**, not maybes. Price the trip on the smallest number you are sure of.
3. **Match the rung to the number.** Find where your per-person budget lands in the tables above — that is your rung.
4. **Check the licence question.** If nobody holds the required licence for your cruising area, bareboat is off the table regardless of budget.
5. **Be honest about the itinerary.** If the plan is three islands in seven days, you need speed — which pushes you up the ladder. If it is one bay and a good book, stay low and spend the saving on a longer trip.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Price two adjacent rungs before deciding. Groups often find the step from bareboat to crewed catamaran smaller per person than expected once ten guests share it — and the holiday is completely different. Run the numbers on confirmed guests, not hopeful ones: the rung that fits the eight people who have actually said yes is the honest answer.
</Callout>
