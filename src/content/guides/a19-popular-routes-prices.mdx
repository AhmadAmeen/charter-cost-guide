"How much is a private jet from New York to Miami?" is one of the most searched questions in private aviation — and the honest answer is a range, not a number. This guide shows how route prices are built, gives you a simple estimator you can apply to any route, and checks it against published broker prices for two of the busiest US corridors.

For the full breakdown of hourly rates by aircraft size, see our [private jet charter cost guide](/private-jets/cost/).

## How route prices are built

Every route quote starts from the same formula: **billable flight hours × the aircraft's hourly rate**, plus everything else. The "everything else" is where quotes diverge:

- **Positioning** — flying the aircraft empty to your departure airport (and sometimes back). On short routes this can exceed the flight time itself.
- **Landing, handling and ramp fees** — set by the airports, higher at congested fields.
- **Crew costs** — overnights if the trip strands the crew away from base.
- **Catering, Wi-Fi and ground transport** — usually itemised separately.
- **Taxes** — air transportation taxes vary by country and route.

Because of all this, two brokers quoting the same city pair can differ by 20 to 30%. The estimator below gets you into the right zone; only an all-in quote tells you the price.

A worked illustration of why positioning matters: take a 55-minute Los Angeles to Las Vegas hop on a light jet at $5,000 per hour. The flight itself is about $4,600 of airtime — but if the aircraft is based an hour away, you may also pay for an hour of empty positioning each way. Suddenly the "55-minute flight" bills nearly three hours, or about $14,000 before fees. Nothing shady happened; you simply paid for the jet to come to you. This is why the positioning question in the tip box above is the highest-value question in private aviation.

## The estimator: flight hours × hourly range

Published broker guides put light jets at roughly **$3,800 to $7,000 per flight hour** (BlackJet cites $3,800–$5,000; Haute Jets lists $6,000–$7,000 on its route pages — sources disagree, so the honest band is wide). Midsize jets sit around $5,500 to $8,500 per hour. The method:

1. Find the published flight time for your route and aircraft size.
2. Convert to hours (2h 45m = 2.75 hours).
3. Multiply by the hourly band.
4. Treat the result as a **floor** — real quotes add the items above, and short routes often carry minimum charges.

## Sourced route examples

**New York → Miami** is the busiest private jet corridor in the US. Published 2026 numbers: Haute Jets lists a light jet at 2h 45m from $19,250, with estimates of $12,000–$18,000 (light), $18,000–$25,000 (midsize) and $22,000–$30,000 (super midsize). Paramount Business Jets, with prices verified August 2026, quotes $17,000–$27,000 one way across categories — light jets from $17,000, midsize from $23,000 — with a flight time of about 2h 20m. The spread between the two brokers is normal: different aircraft, different base locations, different dates.

**Los Angeles → Las Vegas** is the classic short hop: about 55 minutes by light jet (Haute Jets), with published pricing from $8,500. A turboprop does it in about 1h 30m from $6,500 — worth knowing, because on sub-two-hour routes a turboprop can halve the bill while adding only half an hour.

Notice that the flight time barely changes — 2h 50m down to 2h 10m — while the price roughly triples. You are paying for cabin size, speed and prestige, not for getting there much faster. Right-sizing the aircraft to your group is the single biggest lever on any route price: six passengers do not need a fourteen-seat heavy jet.

<Callout type="tip" label="The per-person flip">
Divide each row by a full cabin and the ranking inverts. A $15,000 light jet with six aboard is $2,500 per person; a $32,000 heavy jet with fourteen aboard is about $2,285 per person. Big groups should price big aircraft — the per-seat maths often surprises.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" label="The positioning question">
Before accepting any route price, ask: "Is the aircraft based at my departure airport, and how many positioning hours am I paying for?" On a 55-minute flight, one positioning hour can nearly double the cost. This single question explains most mysteriously high short-route quotes.
</Callout>

## One-way, round trip, and multi-leg

Published route prices are almost always one-way. What you pay for the return depends on what the aircraft does while you are away:

- **Same-day return.** The jet waits for you. You pay waiting time (often a reduced hourly or daily rate) instead of a second full positioning cycle — this is usually the lowest-cost way to do a day trip.
- **Multi-day stay.** The jet typically returns to base and comes back for you, which means two more positioning legs on your invoice. On a 2h 45m route, that can add the equivalent of another one-way fare.
- **Multi-leg itineraries.** Each additional stop adds flight time plus potential waiting and crew overnight costs. Price these as separate legs and add them up — the estimator works per leg.

<Callout type="note" label="Estimator recap">
Flight hours × $3,800–$7,000 (light jet) or × $5,500–$8,500 (midsize) gives a realistic floor for a one-way trip. Then add positioning, fees, taxes and any waiting time — or skip the arithmetic and ask two brokers for all-in quotes on the identical brief.
</Callout>

## How to get an exact quote

1. **Fix the brief.** Same airports (including which private terminal/FBO), dates, times and passenger count for every broker.
2. **Ask two or three brokers or operators.** The spread is routinely 20 to 30% on identical briefs.
3. **Demand the all-in total.** Aircraft, crew, fuel, positioning, landing and handling, catering, taxes — itemised or included, but visible.
4. **Check the aircraft.** Specific type and age, not just "light jet". A 30-year-old airframe and a new one quote differently.
5. **Read the cancellation terms.** What do you owe if you cancel, and what happens if the operator moves or cancels the flight?
