Group charters fail on logistics, not on luxury. The yacht or jet is the easy part; the hard part is eight people, four opinions about money, and one passport expiring in March. This checklist breaks the planning into seven jobs in order, with a week-by-week timeline you can follow as a suggestion rather than a rule.

New to chartering itself? Read our step-by-step guides first: [how to charter a yacht](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/) and [how to charter a private jet](/private-jets/how-to-charter-a-private-jet/).

## Choose a lead organiser

Every successful group trip has one person in charge of decisions and money. Not a committee — one person. The organiser collects preferences, runs the quotes, holds the spreadsheet and is the single contact for the broker or operator. Everyone else's job is to answer promptly and pay on time.

Agree two things up front: how decisions get made (organiser decides after hearing views is the model that works) and a response deadline for each decision, so one slow reply does not stall the whole plan.

## Agree the budget and payment split

Talk money before looking at yachts or jets. It is the conversation groups avoid and the one that causes nearly every blow-up.

1. **Set the per-person ceiling first.** Ask each person privately for the maximum they are comfortable spending, all-in. Plan to the lowest number anyone names — a trip someone resents paying for is a failed trip.
2. **Budget the all-in total, not the headline.** For yachts, the realistic total is roughly 1.4 times the base fee once APA, taxes and tip are added (see [the cost breakdown](/yacht-charter/cost/)). For jets, budget the all-in hourly total including positioning, fees and taxes (see [jet costs](/private-jets/cost/)).
3. **Write down the split method.** Equal shares is simplest and causes the fewest arguments. If yacht cabins differ a lot in size, some groups weight shares — fine, but agree it in writing before money moves.
4. **Write down the dropout rule.** Deposits are usually non-refundable. Agree now: a dropout finds a replacement or forfeits their share. This conversation is painless in week 12 and poisonous in week 2.

<Callout type="tip" label="Illustrative budget split, not a quote">
Say the group agrees on a $70,000 all-in yacht week (an illustrative figure built from the cost layers in our pricing guide). Eight guests splitting equally pay $8,750 each. If two guests take the large master cabins and the group agrees a 1.25x weighting for those cabins, the maths changes — run the exact numbers in your spreadsheet before collecting a cent.
</Callout>

## Decide destination and dates

With the budget set, pick where and when. Two practical filters beat endless debate:

- **Season first, destination second.** A fixed budget goes further in shoulder season. Our [best time to charter by destination](/planning/best-time-to-charter-by-destination/) guide walks through Mediterranean and Caribbean seasons month by month.
- **Travel time matters.** A group flying in from different cities should weigh total door-to-door time, not just the charter itself. A private jet can collapse a two-flight commercial journey into one direct hop — see [when a jet beats commercial for groups](/private-jets/group-travel-vs-commercial/).

Lock the dates with a poll and a deadline. "Sometime in July" is not a date.

## Collect passport and ID details

Do this early — it is the most common last-minute disaster. For each traveller, collect: full name exactly as on the passport, passport number, expiry date, date of birth, nationality, and any dietary or medical notes the crew or caterer needs.

Check two things the moment you have the details:

- **Expiry dates.** Many countries require passports valid at least six months beyond the trip dates, and some airlines will deny boarding if the requirement is not met (source: U.S. Department of State guidance, reported October 2026). Anyone cutting it close should renew now, not in week 3.
- **Visas and entry rules.** Check the destination country's official entry requirements for every nationality in the group — they differ, and the organiser should not assume.

Keep one secure shared document, not a group chat full of passport photos.

## Get quotes and compare

Ask two or three brokers or operators for quotes on the same itinerary: same dates, same group size, same expectations. Compare them line by line — base fee, APA or extras, taxes, positioning, cancellation terms, payment schedule — not by the headline number. Our first-timer guides include quote-comparison sheets you can reuse: [yacht version](/yacht-charter/how-to-charter-a-yacht/) and [jet version](/private-jets/how-to-charter-a-private-jet/).

<Callout type="note" label="One contact per provider">
Have only the lead organiser talk to brokers. Three group members asking the same broker for quotes on the same dates creates confusion and can even push the price up if the broker thinks demand is hot.
</Callout>

## Contract and deposits

Read the contract before anyone pays. The organiser should check: the payment schedule and deposit amounts, cancellation terms in writing, what happens in bad weather, and (for jets) the aircraft substitution policy. If anything is unclear, ask the broker to confirm it in writing — a verbal promise is not a term.

Only then collect deposits. Keep every payment traceable (bank transfer or card, never cash to an individual), log each one in the spreadsheet the day it arrives, and send the group a running total so trust stays high.

Consider [travel insurance](/planning/charter-travel-insurance-checklist/) at the deposit stage: cancellation cover is bought against the money you have already committed, so the sooner deposits are paid, the sooner the cover question matters.

## Final preparations

The last week is admin, not planning:

- Reconfirm headcount, arrival times and meeting points with the operator.
- Send the preference sheet (yacht) or catering and baggage requests (jet) by the operator's deadline.
- Share the operator's emergency contact with the whole group.
- Check travel insurance is in place for everyone, or confirm who has declined it in writing.
- Agree the tip plan: yacht crew tips are commonly around 10% of the base fee, usually handed to the captain at the end; jet tipping norms are looser (see [the tipping guide](/private-jets/tipping-guide/)).

## The week-by-week timeline

A suggestion, not a rule — compress it for off-season or simple trips.

| When | Job |
|------|-----|
| 12 weeks before | Appoint the lead organiser; agree decision process and response deadlines |
| 11 weeks before | Agree per-person budget ceiling, split method and dropout rule — in writing |
| 10 weeks before | Fix destination and dates by poll with a deadline |
| 9 weeks before | Collect all passport and ID details; check expiry dates and visa rules |
| 7–8 weeks before | Get two or three quotes on identical itineraries; compare line by line |
| 6 weeks before | Read the contract; confirm cancellation, weather and substitution terms in writing |
| 5 weeks before | Collect deposits; log every payment; look into travel insurance |
| 3 weeks before | Pay balances per the schedule; send preference sheets and special requests |
| 1 week before | Reconfirm headcount, arrival times and meeting point; share emergency contacts |
| Departure day | Arrive early; organiser carries the contract, payment confirmations and ID copies |

Groups that follow this order rarely fight about money, because every money conversation happened before anyone was emotionally committed to a specific yacht or jet. That is the whole trick.
