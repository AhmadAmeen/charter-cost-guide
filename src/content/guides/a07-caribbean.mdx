The Caribbean looks like one destination from a brochure and behaves like several once you see the invoices. Base rates for the same yacht barely differ between island groups — but taxes, permits and contract terms do, and they can move the final bill by 10% or more. This guide compares the two regions first-timers ask about most — the Bahamas and the Grenadines — with published 2026 numbers, then covers seasons and planning.

For the full breakdown of how charter pricing works anywhere, see our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/). For when to go, see our [month-by-month season guide](/planning/best-time-to-charter-by-destination/).

The headline finding, from a September 2026 rate analysis of 44–50 ft crewed catamarans: base rates across the Grenadines, Bahamas, BVI and USVI sit within 4.3% of each other — $23,000 to $24,000 per week. Everything that follows is about what gets added on top, because that is where the real differences live.

## The regions: Bahamas and Grenadines

**The Bahamas** — the Exumas chain especially — is the bucket-list pick: swimming pigs, Thunderball Grotto, gin-clear shallows. It is also the priciest Caribbean charter week, and the reason is tax, not the boat (source: VitalCharters, September 2026).

**The Grenadines** (St. Vincent and the Grenadines) is the quieter, wilder alternative: the Tobago Cays, Bequia, open-water passages between islands. Smaller fleet, fewer operators, less name recognition — and the lowest landed cost of the four main markets (source: VitalCharters, September 2026). Note it suits second or third charters better than first ones: the sailing is more demanding, with open-water passages and fewer protected anchorages. The reward is anchorages you may have entirely to yourself — the Tobago Cays without six other charter cats beside you is the experience brokers here sell (source: VitalCharters).

Two other regions for context, with published medians from the same rate analysis: the **British Virgin Islands** ($23,500/week median base, no charter tax, but cruising permits around $750 for six guests) is the usual entry point because 61 boats work these waters — the widest choice anywhere, from a fleet of roughly 685 charter vessels, an estimated 40% of the Caribbean's professional charter fleet (source: VitalCharters, September 2026); the **US Virgin Islands** ($24,000 median base, no charter tax) sits close behind.

Two things to read off this table. First, the $3,243 landed gap is almost entirely one line: the Bahamas charter tax ($3,220 of it). Strip that out and the four main Caribbean markets land within about 4% of each other — the choice between them is a choice of sailing ground, not of budget (source: VitalCharters). Second, the APA row: many Caribbean crewed catamarans run **all-inclusive** contracts (CYBA terms) with no separate APA at all; where plus-expenses terms apply, APA of 25–40% is more common on Bahamas motor yachts (source: VitalCharters). Always check which contract your quote uses — it changes the arithmetic completely.

The contract type also decides *how* you pay the government charges, not just whether. Under CYBA all-inclusive terms, cruising taxes, permits and clearance taxes sit inside the rate — a BVI cruising permit never reaches your invoice. Under MYBA plus-expenses terms, local taxes and customs formalities are listed among the expenses the charterer pays at cost, so the identical permit comes out of your APA instead. The Bahamas 14% sits outside the rate on both contract types (source: VitalCharters, MYBA vs CYBA explainer).

## Taxes: where the Bahamas bill grows

This is the section that saves readers the most money, so here it is plainly:

- **Bahamas: 14% charter tax** — 10% VAT plus a 4% charter tax, collected together by the Port Department (from January 1, 2026, under S.I. No. 57 of 2025). On a $23,000 base week that is $3,220; on a $50,000 charter it is $7,000 (source: VitalCharters, September 2026).
- **Grenadines: none.** No charter tax and no VAT on yacht charters embarking in St. Vincent and the Grenadines (source: VitalCharters; BoatCrowd embarkation-tax tables list 0%).
- **On top of tax**, the Bahamas layers a cruising permit ($150–$600 for 30 days, or $500–$3,000 for 12 months, by vessel length — revised April 1, 2026), a $30 passenger tax per non-resident guest beyond three, and Exuma Cays park moorings (sources: GetBoat, September 2026; VitalCharters). The yacht usually absorbs the permit inside the rate or APA; park fees and passenger taxes reach the guest.
- **Grenadines guest fees** are near-negligible: a cruising licence around $46, cruise tax around $13 per person per month, and Tobago Cays Marine Park entry plus moorings totalling about $67 for six guests over a week (source: VitalCharters).

The Bahamas tax sits outside the rate on both common contract types — it is added on top of everything (source: VitalCharters). And it compounds in a way first-timers miss: APA is calculated on the pre-tax base and gratuity is typically calculated on the base too, while the 14% sits on top of the lot. On a $50,000 charter, that is $7,000 in taxes alone before you have tipped the crew (source: VitalCharters).

## Seasons and weather

The Caribbean charter year splits into three (source: LuxuryAchtVacation, September 2026):

- **Peak: December to April.** Steady 15–25 knot northeast trades, 75–84°F, the driest months. February and March are the finest of all. Christmas and New Year weeks book 10–12 months out, often with festive surcharges.
- **Shoulder: May, June and November.** About 80–90% of peak conditions at 30–40% lower rates, quieter anchorages, the warmest water of the year.
- **Off-season: August to October.** Lowest rates (30–50% below peak) but hurricane season, and most fleets reposition. Summer charters stay viable south of the main hurricane belt — including Grenada and the Grenadines.

**Hurricane season officially runs June 1 through November 30** (source: NOAA, May 2026 outlook), with peak activity typically around September 10. Our [month-by-month season guide](/planning/best-time-to-charter-by-destination/) maps every month for both the Caribbean and the Mediterranean.

## Planning notes

- **First charter? Start with the BVI or the Bahamas.** Shorter passages, line-of-sight navigation, deep charter infrastructure. Graduate to the Grenadines when you want wilder water.
- **Book the Grenadines 6–12 months ahead for peak season.** The fleet is a few dozen boats against several hundred in the BVI; most yachts reposition into Grenadines waters for confirmed bookings rather than sitting at a home port (source: VitalCharters). Across the Caribbean, regular peak-season weeks tend to fill 9–12 months ahead, with Christmas, New Year and Easter committed 12–18 months out (source: Waypoints, 2027 charter planner).
- **Budget the tax first in the Bahamas.** Add the 14% before you fall in love with a yacht — it is the single biggest line after the base rate.
- **Confirm the contract type.** All-inclusive (no APA) versus plus-expenses (APA 25–40%) changes your cash flow and your final total. Get it in writing.
- **Read the storm policy.** If you book June–November anywhere in the region, know the operator's hurricane contingency before you pay a deposit.
