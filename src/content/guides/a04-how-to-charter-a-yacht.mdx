Learning how to charter a yacht for the first time is mostly a matter of sequence: decide the trip, set an honest budget, pick the right yacht type, compare quotes carefully and read the contract before you pay. This guide walks through each step in order, so nothing important gets skipped.

If you have not yet worked out what a charter costs, read our [yacht charter cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) alongside this page — it explains the base fee, APA, taxes and tips with worked examples.

## Decide the trip: guests, dates, region

Before you look at a single yacht, answer three questions:

- **Who is coming?** Count every guest, including children. The guest count sets the minimum number of cabins you need and, on some yachts, the legal limit for overnight guests.
- **When can you travel?** Note your exact dates and how flexible they are. Shifting by a week can change both price and availability, especially in peak season.
- **Which region?** The Mediterranean (for example [Greece, Croatia or Italy](/yacht-charter/greece-vs-croatia-vs-italy/)), the Caribbean, the Bahamas and many other areas each have their own season, cruising style and tax rules. Pick the region before the yacht, because the region narrows the fleet.

<Callout type="tip" label="Tip">
Write these three answers down and send the same brief to every broker you contact. Identical briefs produce comparable quotes.
</Callout>

## Set a realistic budget: base fee plus APA, tax and tip

A first-timer's most common mistake is budgeting for the advertised weekly rate alone. A crewed charter has four cost layers:

1. **Base fee** — the weekly hire of the yacht and crew. Indicative range: about $10,000 per week for a small sailing yacht or catamaran to $100,000+ for a large motor yacht (source: YachtCharterFleet, data collected 13 October 2026).
2. **APA (Advance Provisioning Allowance)** — a running-costs deposit, typically about 25 to 40% of the base fee, which pays for fuel, food, drink and dockage during your trip.
3. **Taxes** — local charter taxes or VAT, which vary by country. Published samples cite 22% in Italy, 20% in France and 13% in Croatia (source: BoatCrowd, data collected 13 October 2026).
4. **Crew tip** — around 10% of the base fee is commonly suggested, lower on very large yachts.

A useful rule of thumb: plan for a total of roughly 1.4 times the base fee. Our [cost guide](/yacht-charter/cost/) shows three worked examples with the arithmetic laid out.

## Choose the yacht type

The three main types suit different trips:

- **Sailing yacht** — quieter cruising under sail, usually the lowest base fees. Good for sailors and smaller groups who enjoy the sailing itself.
- **Catamaran** — wide, stable decks and shallow draught for anchoring close to beaches. A popular first-timer choice for families and groups.
- **Motor yacht** — speed and space: you cover more coastline per day, with more deck and cabin volume. Running costs (and therefore APA) run higher — about 35% for motor yachts versus nearer 25% for many crewed catamarans (source: LuxuryCharterGroup, data collected 13 October 2026).

Match the type to your trip from step one: a group of eight island-hopping slowly has different needs from four guests crossing long distances quickly. If you want a bareboat (no crew) charter instead, you will need the licence required for the cruising area — confirm this before you fall in love with a particular boat.

## Ask two or three brokers for quotes

A yacht charter broker compares yachts across fleets, checks real availability and handles the paperwork. For a first charter, contact two or three brokers rather than one, and give each of them the identical trip brief from step one.

Ask each broker for:

- The yacht's full specification and year of last refit
- The base fee for your exact dates
- The APA percentage they recommend for your itinerary
- Which taxes apply and how they are calculated
- What is included and what is billed as an extra
- The payment schedule and cancellation terms

Requesting a quote is normally free. Never pay a deposit before you have a written agreement.

### What to ask about the yacht itself

The brochure photo is a starting point, not the full picture. For each shortlisted yacht, ask:

- **Year built and year of last refit.** A 15-year-old yacht with a recent refit can be a better experience than a newer one that has been worked hard.
- **Cabin layout.** "Sleeps eight" can mean four equal doubles or a mix of doubles, twins and bunks — get the actual layout, especially with children or older guests.
- **Air conditioning and stabilisers.** Worth confirming if anyone in your group is sensitive to heat or motion.
- **Water toys included.** Paddleboards and snorkelling gear are often included; jet skis, seabobs and similar frequently cost extra.
- **Crew number and languages spoken.** More crew generally means smoother service; language matters for the preference sheet conversation.

A broker who answers these quickly and precisely is usually a broker who knows their fleet.

## Compare quotes line by line

Lay the quotes side by side and compare each line, not just the headline weekly rate. A lower base fee can hide a higher APA percentage, excluded taxes or extras billed separately.

If two quotes for similar yachts differ sharply, ask why. Sometimes the cheaper quote excludes items the other includes; sometimes it is simply a better fit for your dates.

## Read the contract and payment schedule

Read the whole agreement before you sign or pay. At minimum, confirm:

- The exact yacht, dates and embarkation port
- The total base fee and the currency it is quoted in
- The APA amount, what it may be spent on, and how unspent funds are returned to you
- The deposit amounts and when each is due
- The cancellation terms and what happens in bad weather
- Which law governs the agreement

<Callout type="warn" label="Warning">
Never pay a deposit without a signed written agreement. If any term is unclear, ask the broker to explain it in writing before you commit.
</Callout>

This is general information, not legal advice. For anything you do not understand, ask the broker — or an independent adviser — before signing.

## Prepare the preference sheet

A few weeks before departure, the crew will ask for a preference sheet: food and drink likes and dislikes, allergies, medical notes, celebration details and the pace you want (busy days versus slow anchorages). Treat it seriously — it is how the chef provisions and how the captain plans the itinerary.

**Preference sheet checklist:**

- Full names and ages of all guests
- Food allergies, intolerances and dietary requirements
- Medical conditions or mobility needs the crew should know about
- Alcohol, wine and soft drink preferences
- Breakfast, lunch and dinner style (light versus formal, local cuisine versus familiar)
- Water sports interests (note that some toys carry an extra charge — ask)
- Special occasions during the trip (birthdays, anniversaries)
- Preferred daily pace: early starts and long cruises, or slow mornings at anchor
- Must-visit places and places to avoid, if you have them
- Emergency contact for each guest

Send it back by the deadline the crew sets. Late or vague sheets mean generic provisioning.

## Embarkation day

Arrive at the time your broker or captain confirms. The crew will show you the yacht, run through the safety briefing and confirm the itinerary. Before leaving the dock:

- Check that the cabins, bathrooms and air conditioning all work
- Confirm the APA process: who holds it and how spending is reported to you
- Save the captain's contact details and the broker's emergency number
- Ask about the plan if the weather turns

Then hand the week to the crew. You did the careful work up front — that is what makes the rest feel effortless.

## After the trip: APA reconciliation

The charter is not quite finished when you disembark. Within the period stated in your agreement, you should receive:

- A statement of how the APA was spent, broken down by category (fuel, food and drink, dockage, other)
- Receipts or supporting records for the spending
- The return of any unspent balance

Read the statement when it arrives. Small discrepancies are usually just categorisation, but anything you do not recognise is worth one polite question to the broker. Keep your copy of the agreement and the APA statement together until the balance is settled — usually a matter of weeks, not months.

## A simple pre-trip timeline

There is no single correct booking lead time; it depends on the season, region and yacht. Use these phases as a guide, and ask your broker what is realistic for your dates:

- **As soon as you decide:** booking early is wise in peak months, when sought-after yachts and weeks go first.
- **Once booked:** read the contract, diarise the payment schedule and note the cancellation terms.
- **In the weeks before:** return the preference sheet by the crew's deadline and confirm arrival logistics.
- **Embarkation day:** arrive on time, attend the safety briefing and confirm the APA process.
