A private jet looks like the most expensive way to travel until you divide by eight. Because charter pricing is per flight hour — not per seat — every extra passenger lowers the per-person cost while the total stays flat. This guide does that division for you, then covers the two things that decide more trips than price: time and baggage.

The hourly bands below come from our [private jet charter cost guide](/private-jets/cost/): light jets $3,500 to $6,500 per hour, midsize jets $4,500 to $8,500, large-cabin jets $7,000 to $13,000, all before fees and taxes.

## Seats by jet category

Seat counts vary by exact model, but the categories in our cost guide run: **light jets 6–8 seats**, **midsize jets 7–9 seats**, **large-cabin/heavy jets 10–16 seats**. One baggage guide breaks it down similarly — light jets 4–6 passengers, midsize 6–9, heavy 10–16 — and notes that individual models differ (source: GlobalCharter).

The practical point: a "light jet" is not automatically right for six people with six suitcases. Seats and baggage are separate constraints, and baggage is usually the tighter one — see below.

One more wrinkle: the seat count is for the cabin, but not every seat is equally usable on every trip. Very light jets seat 2–4 and light jets 4–6 on some models (source: GlobalCharter), so if your group is at the top of a category, confirm the exact aircraft — a "light jet" that seats four will not take your party of six no matter what the brochure category says.

How to use this table: take the per-person figure for your group size and compare it against the airline fare each person would actually pay — business or first class is the honest comparison, not the cheapest economy seat. We do not publish airline fares here because they move daily; plug in your own quotes.

Two patterns to notice. First, the per-person cost roughly halves going from 4 to 8 passengers — filling the cabin is the whole game. Second, the ranges are wide because the underlying hourly rates are wide; your real quote lands somewhere inside, and positioning time can add billed hours (see our cost guide).

<Callout type="note" label="Costs that don't divide">
The table divides the flight hours evenly, but not every cost behaves that way. Positioning flights, overnight crew charges and catering minimums are fixed per trip — they do not shrink when two people drop out. If your group size is uncertain, price the trip at the smallest headcount you might actually fly with.
</Callout>

## Time and airport factors

For many groups, time — not money — is what tips the decision. The published claims:

- **Arrive 15–30 minutes before departure** at the private terminal (FBO) instead of 2–3 hours at a commercial terminal, with private security screening taking 5–20 minutes (source: ACC Aviation).
- **Average saving of 127 minutes per flight** over commercial aviation through streamlined processes and direct routing, per industry research cited by the same operator (source: ACC Aviation).
- **Smaller airports, closer to the destination.** Charter flights can use airfields with no scheduled service, cutting the ground transfer at one or both ends.

Time also compounds: no connections to miss, departure set by the client (the aircraft waits if a meeting runs over), and the whole group boards in minutes. On a multi-leg business day, these savings stack — one operator notes clients call the pre-flight time saving the single biggest benefit, "not the flight itself" (source: ACC Aviation).

The airport experience itself is part of the saving. Private passengers are screened by the pilots or private security officers right before boarding — no shoe removal, no queuing with hundreds of strangers — and walk straight from the terminal door to the aircraft (source: ACC Aviation). For a group, that also removes the herding problem: no stragglers lost between check-in, security and the gate, because there is no gate.

## Baggage and special items

This is where groups get surprised. The rule of thumb from one charter broker: **one standard 23 kg item of luggage per passenger on light and midsize jets** — roughly 6 bags on a light jet, 8 on a midsize, 20 on a large jet with 12 passengers (source: Paramount Business Jets). A second guide gives similar bands: light jets 5–7 medium bags, midsize 7–9 large bags (source: CharterWind, via GlobalCharter's category breakdown).

Practical consequences for groups:

- **Eight people with eight large suitcases** can exceed a light jet's hold — you may need a midsize even though the seats fit.
- **Skis, golf clubs and strollers** are the classic problem items: long, rigid, and often unable to fit through a light jet's cargo door. One guide advises upgrading to at least a midsize jet for golf clubs and skis (source: CharterWind).
- **In-flight access differs.** On many light jets the hold is external — once sealed, no access until landing. Keep medications, documents and valuables in a cabin tote.

Confirm baggage capacity for the specific assigned aircraft before you book, especially with sports equipment.

One more distinction that matters for groups: internal versus external holds. Many private jets split luggage between an internal compartment (accessible during the flight) and an external one — and anything in the external hold is unreachable until landing. Keep medications, documents, valuables and anything anyone might need mid-flight in a small cabin tote per person (source: Charter Flight Group).

## When commercial is simpler

Private aviation wins on flexibility, time and privacy. It does not automatically win on price — and sometimes it loses clearly:

- **Small groups on long routes.** For solo travellers and pairs on long-haul, business class wins on cost "by a factor of more than three" in one published comparison; the jet's per-person case needs six-plus passengers on short to medium hops (source: Medium/FlyRovingFleet, May 2026).
- **Simple fixed schedules.** If everyone flies the same major route at a convenient time, airlines do it cheaper with zero planning effort.
- **Heavy or unusual baggage.** Airlines take skis, surfboards and extra bags for a fee; a light jet may not take them at all.
- **Split itineraries.** If half the group flies from different cities, separate airline tickets beat repositioning a jet.
- **Last-minute changes of plan.** Airline tickets can often be rebooked (for a fee); a chartered jet with a signed contract has cancellation terms that bite harder — check them before you sign.

The honest test: price both, value the hours, check the bags. For a full cabin on a 1–3 hour trip, the jet often wins on all three. For two people crossing an ocean, it rarely does.

One last practical note on splitting the bill: agree the split before anyone boards. The per-person math above assumes an even split, but groups often weight it — the organiser who did the work, or whoever takes the smallest seat, paying less. Whatever the formula, write it down in the group chat before departure, not after landing.
